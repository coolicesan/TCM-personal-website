The Complete Guide to Trying for a BabyFrom Preparing and Tracking Your Cycle to When You Should Get Tested

Where do you start when you're trying to conceive? This guide sets out in order what both partners should do beforehand, how to track your menstrual cycle and confirm ovulation, how long to wait before getting tested, the direction of management for common diagnoses, and what Chinese medicine can and cannot do at each stage.

At a glanceTrying for a baby breaks down into five stages: preparing beforehand (both partners, about three months ahead), getting to know your cycle, identifying your fertile window and trying, getting tested if it hasn't worked (after a year if you're under 35, six months if you're over), and deciding the direction of management from the results. The most common mistake is putting "building up your health" ahead of "getting tested" — get the order wrong and you waste the time.

Once you decide to have a baby, information tends to arrive all at once: take folic acid, measure your temperature, work out your ovulation, test your AMH, build up your health... but few people explain clearly what order to do these things in.

This article sets out the whole process in actual time order, with a link under each stage to the detailed explanation.

One variable runs through the whole process: age. After 35, the time you can afford to wait at each stage shortens — this isn't scaremongering but a reality that has to be built into your planning.

Stage 1: Before You Start (About Three Months Ahead)

Why three months?

  • An egg takes about three months to develop from the start until it is released
  • Sperm take over seventy days to develop and mature, which with transit time also comes close to three months
  • Folic acid needs to be started in advance to protect during the critical period when the neural tube forms

In other words, the changes you make now take about three months to show up in the body.

For the woman

  • Folic acid: it's recommended to start before you plan to conceive and to continue into early pregnancy. Those with a particular history or risk factors (for example a previous baby with a neural tube defect, diabetes, or certain medications) may need a higher dose and should check with a doctor first.
  • Basic health checks: blood pressure, weight, blood sugar; whether your rubella (German measles) immunity and cervical screening are up to date.
  • Review your current medications: if you have a long-term condition or take any prescription drugs, ask your doctor first which need to be adjusted — don't stop them yourself.
  • Stop smoking and limit alcohol, control your weight and improve your sleep.
  • A dental check-up: dental problems are more awkward to treat during pregnancy, so it's best to deal with them beforehand.

For the man

This part is often skipped, but the man's preparation also takes three months.

  • Stop smoking, drink less, control weight and improve sleep
  • Reduce prolonged scrotal heat exposure (long periods of sitting, saunas, a laptop on the lap)
  • Stop anabolic steroids (under a doctor's guidance)
  • Review current medications

For details, see Male Fertility: What Affects Sperm Quality, and How Can You Improve It?

Stage 2: Getting to Know Your Cycle

Record first, judge afterwards

Record for at least three months: the first day of each bleed, how many days it lasts, the flow and how bad the period pain is.

The criteria are actually quite clear — a cycle of 24 to 38 days, bleeding within 8 days, and not much difference from month to month. Falling outside the range doesn't necessarily mean illness, but it's worth discussing with your doctor (see How Long Should a Normal Menstrual Cycle Be?).

Confirming whether you ovulate

A regular cycle doesn't necessarily mean you are ovulating. There are three ways to confirm it, each answering a different question:

MethodWarns you in advanceConfirms afterwards
Ovulation test strips (LH)✅❌

The usual combination in practice is: strips or mucus to catch the timing, temperature to verify afterwards (see How Do You Know If You're Ovulating?).

Don't take period pain for granted

If period pain makes you take time off, painkillers work less and less, or it gets worse year by year, that isn't "normal" and should be checked — the average delay in diagnosing endometriosis is measured in years (see How Bad Does Period Pain Have to Be Before It's Not Normal?).

Stage 3: The Trying Phase

The fertile window is the few days before ovulation

Sperm can survive for up to about five days, and an egg lasts only about 12 to 24 hours after it is released. So the best chance is the roughly five days before ovulation plus the day of ovulation — by the time you have sex after ovulation, it's often already too late.

The way to work it out is cycle length minus 14, not counting forward 14 days from this period (see How Do You Calculate Your Ovulation Window?).

No need to calculate the frequency

Once every one to two days during the fertile window is enough. Calculating the dates too precisely tends to pile on stress for both partners, and stress itself affects ovulation and the quality of your sex life — a fairly common vicious circle in practice.

Stage 4: When Should You Get Tested?

SituationWhen to seek help
Woman under 35Not pregnant after trying for 12 months
Woman 35 or overNot pregnant after trying for 6 months
Woman 40 or overSee a doctor straight away
Infrequent periods, no periods for long stretches, severe period painDon't wait
Past pelvic infection, pelvic surgery or endometriosisDon't wait
Male partner has had testicular surgery, an undescended testicle or chemotherapyDon't wait

Two principles:

  • "Let's try a bit longer first" is the most costly decision. The tests themselves aren't painful, but the time lost by delay can't be made up.
  • Both partners must be tested together. The causes are spread roughly evenly between the two, so testing only one means seeing only half the picture. And the semen analysis is the simplest, cheapest and quickest of all the tests.

For the detailed tests and how to read the reports, see Why Can't I Get Pregnant? Common Causes, Which Tests to Have and How Chinese Medicine Can Help.

About assisted reproduction

IUI and IVF are two different levels of treatment. IUI is simpler, cheaper and lighter on the body, but needs open fallopian tubes and has a lower chance of success per cycle; IVF needs an egg retrieval procedure and more intensive medication, but can bypass some structural obstacles.

For the full process, public and private options in Hong Kong and the discussion of PGT, see IVF Step by Step.

The Role of Chinese Medicine in Trying for a Baby

This section needs to be accurate, because wrongly set expectations are the easiest way to waste time.

What it can't do

  • It can't open blocked fallopian tubes — structural problems need surgery or to be bypassed with IVF
  • It can't repair a serious problem with sperm production
  • It can't increase the number of follicles in the ovaries — the number of follicles is set at birth
  • It can't replace tests

Where there is room to help

  • Regulating the menstrual cycle and ovulation: irregular cycles and erratic ovulation are the area Chinese medicine more commonly works on
  • Mood, sleep and stress: these genuinely affect the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, and are the part you feel most directly
  • Easing period pain and related discomfort
  • Working alongside assisted reproduction: meta-analyses of Chinese-language studies suggest that Chinese herbal medicine alongside IVF may reduce the incidence of ovarian hyperstimulation and improve the clinical pregnancy rate, though study quality has room to improve (see How Does Chinese Medicine Work Alongside an IVF Cycle?)
  • The man's overall condition and lifestyle factors

The order matters

Get tested first, then work on your health. If you already know there is a tubal blockage or a serious male factor, working on your health first only wastes time; conversely, if the tests are normal but your cycles are erratic, your sleep is poor and your stress is high, that is exactly where Chinese medicine has room.

Just as important: don't let preparation delay treatment. If your doctor advises starting soon, don't postpone it for the sake of "three months of preparation first" — age is the most irreversible variable.

When to See a Doctor Without Delay

  • You already meet the timing criteria above
  • No period for three months or more (and not pregnant)
  • Cycles persistently longer than 38 days, or fewer than 8 periods a year
  • Period pain that makes you take time off, that painkillers don't touch, or that gets worse year by year
  • Pain during sex, or pain opening your bowels or passing urine during your period
  • Bleeding between periods, or after sex
  • Two or more miscarriages
  • Hot flushes, night sweats or markedly lighter periods before the age of 40
  • Testicular pain, swelling or a lump in the male partner — get seen promptly

Frequently Asked Questions

How far ahead should I start preparing?

Generally about three months. That matches the time it takes for eggs to develop and sperm to be produced, and gives folic acid and lifestyle changes time to take effect.

Does the man need to prepare too?

Yes. About a third of infertility is linked to the male partner, and sperm production also takes about three months. The man's preparation shouldn't wait until the woman's tests are done.

How long should we try before getting tested?

A year if you're under 35, six months if you're 35 or over, and seeing a doctor straight away if you're 40 or over. If you already know of a menstrual problem, a history of pelvic surgery or a relevant history in the male partner, don't wait.

Should I see a Chinese medicine practitioner first, or get tested first?

Get tested first. Once the cause is clear, you know whether Chinese medicine care is appropriate and what it should address.

How long before Chinese medicine care makes a difference?

Generally you think in months, and usually need to observe two to three menstrual cycles to judge the direction. But that timetable has to be considered alongside the overall treatment plan, and you shouldn't postpone treatment that has already been advised for the sake of preparation.

Does stress really affect getting pregnant?

Yes. Long-term stress and lack of sleep affect the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis and may make ovulation irregular, and they also affect your sex life and general state. Working out the dates too precisely is itself one of the common sources of stress.

After what age should I stop waiting?

There's no absolute cut-off, but after 35 egg quality and numbers decline faster, so the time you can afford at each decision should be shortened. If you are near or past 35, we suggest swapping the thought of "observing a few more months" for "get tested first, then decide".

Conclusion

The order for trying to conceive isn't complicated: prepare three months ahead (both partners) → get to know your cycle → identify your fertile window → if it hasn't worked when the time is up, get tested together → decide the direction from the results.

There are only two common mistakes: only the woman getting tested, and putting building up your health ahead of testing. Avoid those two, and every decision after that will be well spent.

Chinese medicine has a place in regulating the cycle, mood and sleep, easing period pain and working alongside assisted reproduction, but it should sit inside the overall plan, not ahead of it.
