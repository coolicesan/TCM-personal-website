How Bad Does Period Pain Have to Be Before It's Not Normal?Causes, Tests and When to See a Doctor

Period pain that makes you take time off, that painkillers don't touch, or that gets worse year after year is not "normal". A Hong Kong registered Chinese medicine practitioner explains the difference between primary and secondary dysmenorrhoea, why endometriosis is so often diagnosed late, which tests to have, and how TCM approaches it.

At a glancePeriod pain is common in itself, but pain bad enough to take time off, that painkillers don't touch, that gets worse year by year, or that happens outside your period is no longer something to keep putting up with. These can point to endometriosis, adenomyosis or fibroids, and should be checked. The line "period pain is normal for women" has left a lot of women in pain for years for no good reason.

Period pain is the gynaecological symptom most often brushed aside — because it is so widespread that everyone takes it for granted. But "common" doesn't mean "normal", and it doesn't mean it needs no attention.

Below we start with how to tell the two kinds of period pain apart, then the possible causes behind it and the tests you should have.

Two Kinds of Period Pain, Managed Completely Differently

Primary dysmenorrhoeaSecondary dysmenorrhoea
DefinitionNo other underlying diseaseCaused by another gynaecological problem
When it usually startsWithin a year or two of the first periodUsually appears at a later age
When it hurtsFrom a day or two before the period to the first two daysMay last the whole period, or even occur outside it
Change with ageMostly eases with age or after having childrenTends to get worse year by year
Response to painkillersUsually worksWorks less and less, or not at all
CauseProstaglandins making the uterus contract stronglyEndometriosis, adenomyosis, fibroids, pelvic inflammatory disease and others

The key clue to telling them apart is the "trend": primary dysmenorrhoea is usually steady or easing; if your pain is getting worse year on year, that is a signal to get checked.

When Is It Not Normal?

If any one of the following applies, don't treat it as something you can just get through:

  • The pain is bad enough that you have to take time off, and can't work or go to school
  • Ordinary painkillers don't work, or you need more and more of them
  • The pain is getting worse year by year
  • The pain also happens outside your period
  • Pain during sex
  • Pain when opening your bowels or passing urine during your period
  • It comes with a marked increase in flow or large clots
  • It comes with difficulty conceiving

Of these, pain during sex and pain opening your bowels during your period are particularly important — they are classic clues to endometriosis, yet few people mention them to their doctor.

Common Secondary Causes

Endometriosis: tissue like the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterine cavity, bleeds with each cycle but cannot escape, and causes inflammation and adhesions. A cyst that forms on the ovary is commonly called a "chocolate cyst".

Adenomyosis: tissue like the uterine lining grows into the muscle wall of the uterus. The typical picture is worsening period pain together with heavier flow.

Fibroids: depending on their position and size, they can cause heavier flow, period pain and a sense of pressure.

Pelvic inflammatory disease: a past or current infection causing adhesions and chronic pelvic pain.

An intrauterine device (IUD): some women have worse period pain in the early period after it is fitted.

Endometriosis: The One Most Often Diagnosed Late

This section deserves special attention, because it is the most common cause behind period pain and the one most often left too long.

How common it is: it is estimated to affect about one in ten women of reproductive age, and the proportion is higher among women with infertility.

Why it's delayed: from the first symptoms to a formal diagnosis, the literature generally records an average delay of about 4 to 11 years. The reasons include varied symptoms, period pain being seen as normal, and the fact that diagnosis used to depend on surgery.

The way it's diagnosed has changed — and that is good news worth knowing. The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) updated its guideline in 2022, and laparoscopy is no longer the gold standard for diagnosis; imaging (transvaginal ultrasound, and MRI where needed) now comes first, and laparoscopy is recommended only when imaging is negative, or when empirical treatment hasn't worked or isn't suitable.

What this means in practice: you don't need to have surgery before management can begin. If you have typical symptoms, you can start with an ultrasound and discuss a management plan with your doctor.

A reminder: the severity of symptoms doesn't necessarily match the extent of the disease — severe pain doesn't necessarily mean severe disease, and a mild-looking scan doesn't mean your pain isn't real.

Which Tests Should You Have?

TestWhat it checks
Detailed historyWhen the pain comes, where it is, what it's like, how it's changing, and any pain during sex or when opening your bowels
Pelvic examinationMobility of the uterus, tender points, masses
Transvaginal ultrasoundFibroids, adenomyosis, ovarian cysts (including chocolate cysts)
MRIWhen the ultrasound is uncertain or deep lesions are suspected
Blood testsAnaemia, thyroid and so on, as appropriate

A record is the most valuable preparation: going to the doctor with three months of pain records (a daily score from 0 to 10, painkiller use, and which activities were affected) is far more useful than describing it as "really painful".

How Does Chinese Medicine View Period Pain?

Chinese medicine calls it "painful menstruation" (tong jing), and pattern differentiation turns on the character of the pain, its timing, and what makes it better or worse — which is exactly why a Chinese medicine consultation asks such detailed questions.

A practical rule of thumb: pain that dislikes pressure is mostly an excess pattern (stasis, cold, damp-heat); pain that likes pressure and feels better for it is mostly a deficiency pattern. Pain before the period is mostly excess; pain after the period is mostly deficiency.

The above is a general introduction to TCM theory, not a diagnosis or a prescription. Herbal treatment needs an in-person consultation and pattern differentiation by a registered Chinese medicine practitioner.

It needs to be said: if the pain comes from a structural problem such as endometriosis, adenomyosis or fibroids, Chinese medicine care can be part of the overall management (for example easing pain, mood and sleep), but it can't replace necessary tests and treatment. Find out the cause first, then decide the direction of care.

What You Can Do Day to Day

  • Apply heat to your lower abdomen or lower back — simple, and many women find it helps
  • Exercise regularly — moderate activity around your period helps circulation, but don't force it
  • Manage sleep and stress — both genuinely affect how pain feels
  • Cut back on cold, raw foods and too much caffeine before your period
  • Keep a record of pain scores — not only for your appointment but also so you can see the trend clearly

About painkillers: using them when you need them isn't "failure", and it won't make your body "get used to" them. Taking them when the pain is just starting usually works better than waiting until it's severe. If you need more and more, or they work less well, that in itself is a sign to see a doctor, not a reason to raise the dose.

When to See a Doctor

  • Period pain that makes you take time off or clearly affects daily life
  • Painkillers that don't work or work less and less
  • Period pain that gets worse year by year
  • Pain during sex, or pain opening your bowels or passing urine during your period
  • Pelvic pain outside your period
  • Markedly heavier flow, large clots, or symptoms of anaemia
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35) (see Why Can't I Get Pregnant?)
  • Sudden severe abdominal pain or fever — seek care immediately, to rule out emergencies such as ovarian torsion or infection

Frequently Asked Questions

Will painkillers for period pain stop working the more I take them?

Ordinary painkillers don't make the body dependent. If you find they work less well or you need to keep increasing the dose, the more likely explanation is an underlying cause that hasn't been dealt with (such as endometriosis); you should be checked, rather than keep adding more medication.

Will period pain get better after I've had a baby?

Primary dysmenorrhoea can indeed ease after childbirth. But if it is secondary — especially adenomyosis or endometriosis — having a baby is no guarantee of improvement, and for some women it becomes more noticeable after the birth.

Do I definitely need surgery for a chocolate cyst?

Not necessarily. Whether to operate depends on the size of the cyst, the severity of symptoms, your plans for having a family and how you respond to medication, and needs to be assessed individually by a gynaecologist. Diagnosis and management today are more conservative than before, and surgery isn't needed as soon as one is found.

Can I see a Chinese medicine practitioner for period pain?

Yes, but we suggest having basic tests first (at least one pelvic ultrasound) to rule out a structural problem before deciding the direction. If endometriosis or adenomyosis is already confirmed, Chinese medicine can be part of the overall management, but should run alongside gynaecological follow-up and not replace it.

Does applying heat really help?

Many women find it helps, and the risk and cost are low, so it's worth trying. From a Chinese medicine angle, it fits the "warm the channels and disperse cold" direction for the cold-congealing-with-stasis pattern — which also explains why heat works especially well for some women and not much for others.

Can I exercise during my period?

Yes — just adjust the intensity to how you feel. Moderate activity helps the period pain of many women, so there's no need to force yourself to stay in bed, but there's no need to push yourself to keep up your usual level either.

Conclusion

What decides whether period pain is normal isn't "how bad it is" but the trend and the impact: is it getting worse? Is it affecting your life? Do painkillers still work? Is there pain during sex or when opening your bowels?

If the answers worry you, it's worth having a check. The average delay in diagnosing endometriosis is measured in years, and surgery is no longer needed before an assessment can begin.

Chinese medicine has a role in easing pain, improving mood and sleep, and general care, but it should rest on the cause already being known.
