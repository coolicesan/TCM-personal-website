How Does Chinese Medicine Work Alongside an IVF Cycle?A Phase-by-Phase Approach and What the Research Shows

Meta-analyses of Chinese-language studies suggest that Chinese herbal medicine alongside IVF may reduce ovarian hyperstimulation, improve endometrial receptivity and raise clinical pregnancy rates. A Hong Kong registered Chinese medicine practitioner explains the phase-by-phase approach — before the cycle, down-regulation, stimulation and after transfer — plus when to time acupuncture and what to watch out for in practice.

At a glanceThe core principle of combining Chinese medicine with IVF is phase-by-phase treatment: the herbal direction at each stage follows what the fertility treatment is trying to achieve at that stage, rather than one formula being used for the whole cycle. Meta-analyses of Chinese-language studies suggest that adding Chinese herbal medicine to IVF may reduce the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS), improve endometrial receptivity and raise clinical pregnancy rates.

"I'm in the middle of IVF — can I see a Chinese medicine practitioner at the same time?" Yes, you can, but what matters is how the two are coordinated. If the herbal direction runs against what your fertility specialist is trying to do at that moment, it won't help and may even work against the treatment. We'll look at the research first, then go through what happens in practice at each phase.

If you'd like to understand the IVF process itself first, see our step-by-step guide to IVF.

What Does the Research Say?

Chinese herbal medicine alongside IVF: a meta-analysis of Chinese-language studies

A 2022 meta-analysis by a team from the School of Chinese Medicine at the University of Hong Kong searched PubMed, the Cochrane Library, Web of Science, CNKI and Wanfang, and pooled 14 randomised controlled trials involving 1,854 women. The results:

OutcomeResult
Incidence of ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS)Significantly lower in the herbal medicine group (OR 0.32, 95% CI 0.24–0.42, P<0.00001)
Number of eggs retrievedMore moderate in the herbal medicine group (MD −2.21)
Clinical pregnancy rateHigher in the herbal medicine group (OR 1.68, 95% CI 1.31–2.16, P<0.0001)

A second meta-analysis, published in the Chinese Journal of Modern Applied Pharmacy, pointed in the same direction: kidney-tonifying herbs combined with conventional medication did better than conventional medication alone at improving ovarian reserve and endometrial receptivity.

The most notable finding: fewer cases of OHSS

Of the three outcomes, the effect on OHSS is the clearest (OR 0.32, meaning the odds of it occurring fell to roughly a third). It is also the easiest to make sense of:

  • It lines up directly with the prescribing principle described below — working with the goal of each phase and not over-stimulating the ovaries
  • It also helps explain the "more moderate number of eggs retrieved": more eggs is not always better, and retrieving too many is itself a risk factor for OHSS
  • It is a safety benefit — reducing complications is worthwhile in its own right

In other words, Chinese medicine's role here is less about helping things along and more about keeping them steady.

The limitations, stated plainly

The authors of both meta-analyses were explicit about the limits of their evidence. The first noted that the quality of the included studies was low and that the criteria for judging efficacy varied, and called for more high-quality, multicentre, randomised double-blind trials. The second noted that most of the included studies scored only 3 on the Jadad scale, so the evidence behind the conclusions is limited.

It is also worth being clear that the main outcome in these studies was the clinical pregnancy rate (a gestational sac seen on ultrasound), not the live birth rate. A large acupuncture trial that used live birth as its outcome came back negative, so there is currently not enough evidence to support a benefit for live births.

The honest summary is this: the available Chinese-language studies suggest that herbal medicine alongside IVF may reduce OHSS and improve clinical pregnancy rates, but it is a supportive therapy that may help, not a guarantee of success. Anyone who promises you a specific success rate is going beyond what the evidence can support.

Phase-by-Phase Treatment: How to Fit Chinese Medicine to Each Phase

The framework below is drawn from the clinical approach used in Chinese medicine hospitals in mainland China for patients with diminished ovarian reserve (DOR). The core principle is that the Chinese medicine strategy points the same way as the goal of the Western medication at that phase.

Before the cycle starts: where Chinese medicine has the most room

There is an old saying in Chinese medicine: "to conceive, first regulate the menses" — get the menstrual cycle regular first, then think about conception. That makes the preparation period before a cycle the time when Chinese medicine has the most scope in the whole treatment course.

The approach is cycle-regulating herbal therapy, which shifts the emphasis of the prescription across the four phases of the menstrual cycle:

PhaseWhat the body is doingTreatment direction
MenstruationDischarging rather than storing; extreme yang turning to yinNourish and invigorate blood; clear stasis to make way for renewal
Follicular phaseYin growingTonify the kidneys, replenish essence, nourish yin and blood
OvulationStoring rather than discharging; extreme yin turning to yangTonify the kidneys and support yang; invigorate blood and unblock the channels
Luteal phaseYang growingTonify the kidneys and support yang; soothe the liver and strengthen the spleen

Timing: the usual approach in mainland China is about three months of herbal pre-treatment before starting a cycle. A related retrospective observation also suggests that the improvement is more marked with early intervention (women whose cycles are still regular and who have had the problem for a shorter time), and that the trend levels off after about six months.

Two caveats are needed, though. That observation comes from a single-centre retrospective study with no control group, so the level of evidence is limited. And you shouldn't delay your cycle just to finish three months of preparation — the same source itself stresses "keep actively trying to conceive while you regulate your body", and age and time are important variables in their own right.

Down-regulation: keep things calm

In this phase, GnRH analogues put the pituitary and ovaries into something like a temporary shutdown. Common side effects are hot flushes and sweating, an aching lower back and knees, irritability and vaginal dryness — a picture that closely matches what Chinese medicine calls kidney yin deficiency.

The treatment principle is to nourish kidney yin and clear deficiency heat, while also strengthening the spleen, soothing the liver and calming the mind.

What matters most in this phase is what you don't do:

  • Avoid warming, drying and "moving" herbs such as deer antler velvet (lu rong), curculigo (xian mao) and cinnamon bark (rou gui)
  • Avoid strong blood-invigorating herbs such as peach kernel (tao ren) and sparganium rhizome (san leng)
  • The principle is to avoid stirring up ovarian function — because your fertility doctor is deliberately suppressing it at this point

Worth remembering: if a practitioner offers you a "tonic to build you up and produce more eggs" during down-regulation, that runs against the aim of your fertility treatment.

Ovarian stimulation: balance yin and yang

Exogenous gonadotropins bring many follicles to rapid, simultaneous development over a short period, which Chinese medicine understands as using up a large amount of kidney essence and blood in a short time. Treatment therefore centres on tonifying the kidneys and replenishing essence, with some warming of kidney yang, and a small amount of blood-invigorating, channel-unblocking herbs may be added to help the follicles mature.

Interestingly, the yang-warming herbs that were to be avoided during down-regulation, such as epimedium (yin yang huo, also known as xian ling pi), become a common choice in the stimulation phase. This shows the internal logic of the framework: the herbal direction follows the goal of each phase.

This phase is also the critical window for controlling OHSS risk, so the prescription has to balance helping the follicles mature against avoiding an over-response.

Endometrial preparation: tonify the kidneys and invigorate blood

Repeated egg retrievals are thought to deplete kidney yin and yang, leaving the vessels empty and sluggish so that stasis gradually forms and affects the lining's receptivity. This phase typically uses a kidney-tonifying, blood-invigorating approach, aiming to improve endometrial thickness and morphology and lower uterine artery resistance.

The Chinese Journal of Modern Applied Pharmacy meta-analysis mentioned earlier reached its conclusion — that kidney-tonifying herbs did better than conventional medication alone — at this very stage.

After transfer: nurture and settle the pregnancy

Because of the heavy medication used in down-regulation and stimulation, and the loss of granulosa cells during egg retrieval, luteal insufficiency is common at this stage. Chinese medicine's basic direction is to support the kidneys and spleen and secure the chong vessel to protect the pregnancy, working alongside the luteal support your fertility doctor prescribes.

The prescribing principle is "secure and nourish, never dry or heat", and pungent, hot, drying and strongly tonifying herbs such as aconite (fu zi) and cinnamon bark (rou gui) are expressly avoided. What the body needs now is stability and nourishment, not stimulation.

Throughout: emotions and sleep

IVF brings financial, psychological and physical pressure all at once, and anxiety, tension and fear are very common — especially after repeated failed transfers.

Chinese medicine theory holds that for women, "the liver is the root": the liver governs the smooth flow of qi and is closely tied to emotions. The clinical caution is that when emotional strain is being treated, the approach should also soften and nourish the liver and protect liver yin, rather than relying on dispersing alone — people under prolonged pressure often have a yin-and-blood deficiency at the same time, and soothing alone can drain them further.

A gentler day-to-day option is herbal flower tea. Rose bud (mei gui hua), Chinese rose (yue ji hua), albizia flower (he huan hua) and white plum blossom (bai mei hua) are the usual choices for soothing the liver and relieving constraint. Albizia flower also helps calm the mind and sleep, and rose bud is the most effective for lifting low mood.

Even so, check with your doctor before drinking even a flower tea during an IVF cycle — Chinese rose, for example, has a fairly strong blood-invigorating action, and whether it is suitable after transfer has to be judged case by case. "Gentle" doesn't mean "suitable at every phase".

When Should You Have Acupuncture, and How Should Sessions Be Planned?

A sensible way to schedule it

Best done in the gap between cycles. This is the most suitable window: there is no medication in your system, the time pressure is lower, and there is room to work properly on sleep, digestion, emotions and energy. A typical schedule is one session a week, reviewed month by month — it usually takes two to three menstrual cycles to judge whether it is heading in the right direction.

During stimulation: aim to ease discomfort. Sessions can be arranged around whatever is bothering you, such as bloating, insomnia or tension. Once a week is enough. Your body is going through dramatic hormonal changes at this point, so it's best not to pile on too many interventions at the same time.

Around egg retrieval: extra caution. Retrieval involves anaesthesia and a risk of bleeding. Strong stimulating techniques should be avoided before and after it, and any blood-invigorating herbs need a practitioner's assessment. If you do book acupuncture, keep it gentle and make sure your fertility doctor knows.

After transfer: less is more. Strong hands-on treatment is not recommended. If you have marked discomfort or anxiety, light treatment can be considered, but sessions shouldn't be packed close together.

Each session usually lasts 20 to 30 minutes. IVF already involves a lot of appointments and injections, and adding too many sessions increases the load on both your body and your schedule — it's worth weighing that honestly.

Practical Precautions

1. Both practitioners need to know what the other is doing. Your fertility doctor needs to know what you are taking, and your Chinese medicine practitioner needs to know which stimulation drugs and luteal support medication you are on. Keeping either one in the dark raises the risk.

2. Take extra care around egg retrieval. Herbs that invigorate blood and resolve stasis should only be used before and after retrieval on a practitioner's assessment. Don't take them on your own, and especially don't reuse an earlier prescription.

3. Avoid strong stimulation after transfer. And don't take so-called "pregnancy-protecting" formulas sold over the counter on your own. What you take at this stage has to be decided by a practitioner based on your actual situation.

4. Don't add or change medications or supplements on your own. During stimulation your hormones are changing dramatically, so taking products of unknown composition is especially risky.

5. Don't let preparation delay your cycle. Age is one of the most important factors in IVF outcomes. If your doctor advises starting soon, don't postpone it for the sake of "three months of preparation first".

How to Tell Whether a Practitioner Knows Assisted Reproduction

Even if you have no plans to take herbs, this framework has a practical use — it helps you judge whether a practitioner genuinely knows this area:

  • They ask which phase you're in and what medication you're on, and adjust the herbal direction accordingly
  • They don't prescribe the same formula for the whole cycle
  • They don't give you a "big tonic to boost egg production" during down-regulation
  • They don't use warming, drying, strongly tonifying herbs after transfer
  • They're willing to communicate with your fertility doctor, rather than asking you to keep it quiet

Frequently Asked Questions

Can Chinese medicine improve IVF success rates?

Meta-analyses of Chinese-language studies suggest that adding Chinese herbal medicine to IVF may reduce ovarian hyperstimulation, improve endometrial receptivity and raise clinical pregnancy rates. But the quality of the included studies was low, as the authors themselves note. A reasonable way to see it is as something that may help, not a guarantee. Treat any claim that promises a specific success rate with caution.

When is the best time to start?

The preparation period before a cycle is generally seen as the phase where Chinese medicine has the most scope, and the usual approach in mainland China is around three months. But if your doctor advises starting treatment soon, don't postpone it to finish a course of preparation.

Can I take the same herbal formula in every phase?

It isn't recommended. The aims of the Western medication differ a great deal between phases — suppressing the ovaries in down-regulation, stimulating follicles in stimulation, supporting implantation after transfer — and the Chinese medicine direction should change with them. If you are given the same formula for the whole cycle without any changes, ask why.

Can I take herbal medicine during ovarian stimulation?

Yes, but a registered Chinese medicine practitioner needs to assess your situation, and your fertility doctor should know about it. Hormones are changing dramatically during stimulation, so take extra care — don't self-medicate or reuse an old prescription.

Can I have acupuncture after embryo transfer?

That depends on your individual situation and needs a practitioner's judgement. In general, strongly stimulating techniques are not recommended after transfer, and any treatment should be given only with your fertility doctor's knowledge.

How many acupuncture sessions do I need?

There is no set number. If the aim is to look after sleep, digestion, emotions and energy, a typical schedule is one session a week in the gap between cycles, reviewed over two to three menstrual cycles. Be cautious about "package" deals that sell a fixed number of sessions and tie them to success rates.

My first cycle didn't work. How can Chinese medicine help?

The gap between cycles is when Chinese medicine has the most room: working on fatigue, sleep, emotions and overall recovery, and regulating your constitution. It is also a good time to go through the details of the last cycle with your fertility specialist.

Conclusion

What matters about combining Chinese medicine with IVF is not whether you take herbs, but whether the treatment fits the phase. Calm during down-regulation, supportive during stimulation, nourishing after transfer — the direction follows the Western treatment goal, and only then can it be called coordinated care.

On the evidence so far, the clearest signal for herbal medicine alongside IVF is in reducing ovarian hyperstimulation, with positive results for endometrial receptivity and clinical pregnancy rates too, though study quality still has room to improve. Add support for discomfort, sleep and emotions during treatment, and together these make up the role Chinese medicine can play in IVF.

The two most important practical points: make sure both practitioners know your full situation, and don't let preparation delay your treatment.
