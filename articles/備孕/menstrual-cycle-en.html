How Long Should a Normal Menstrual Cycle Be?How to Count It, the Four Phases and Warning Signs

A normal menstrual cycle runs 24 to 38 days, with bleeding of no more than 8 days and a difference of about a week or less from one cycle to the next. A Hong Kong registered Chinese medicine practitioner explains how to count your cycle, what happens in your body in each of the four phases, which signs are abnormal, and how TCM views early, delayed and irregular cycles.

At a glanceThe standard from the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) is: a cycle length of 24 to 38 days, bleeding that lasts 8 days or less, and a difference between one cycle and the next of about a week or less. Falling outside these ranges doesn't necessarily mean illness, but it's worth recording and discussing with your doctor — because your cycle is the most direct signal of how your body is doing.

"My period only comes every 32 days — is that abnormal?" "Last month it was 26 days, this month 34 — is that a big problem?" Whether a period is normal actually has fairly clear criteria; it's just that few people explain them clearly. Below we'll start with how to count your cycle, then how to judge it.

How Do You Count Your Menstrual Cycle?

Cycle length
Counted from the first day of this period's bleeding to the day before the first day of the next period's bleeding.

The two most common ways to get it wrong:

  • ❌ Starting the count from the day the period ends
  • ❌ Counting the day of a little brown spotting as day one

Day one = the first day of clear red bleeding. Brown spotting a day or two before doesn't count and belongs to the previous cycle.

What Counts as Normal?

ItemNormal rangeCalled when outside the range
Cycle length24 to 38 daysFewer than 24 days: frequent; more than 38 days: infrequent
Length of bleeding8 days or fewerMore than 8 days: prolonged
RegularityOver a year, the longest and shortest cycles differ by about a week or lessA difference of more than 20 days: irregular
Amount of bleedingDoesn't feel excessive and doesn't interfere with daily lifeNeeding to change protection every hour, large clots, anaemia

It should be said that you may have seen "21 to 35 days" elsewhere — that is the range used in patient information from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG). Both are in use, and the difference comes from different statistical bases. If your cycle falls between 21 and 24 days, there's no need to panic, but it's worth recording for a few months and then discussing with your doctor.

Another point that is often overlooked: regularity matters more than length. A steady 34-day cycle usually says more about normal ovulation than a cycle that jumps between 26 and 40 days.

When Does Ovulation Happen? A Key Fact

Many people assume ovulation always happens on "day 14". That is only true of a 28-day cycle.

What actually happens is that the length of the luteal phase is relatively fixed, generally about 12 to 16 days; differences in cycle length come mainly from differences in the follicular phase.

In other words:

  • 28-day cycle → ovulation around day 14
  • 35-day cycle → ovulation around day 21
  • 24-day cycle → ovulation around day 10

So to estimate ovulation, count back about 14 days from your next period, rather than forward 14 days from this one. This is also why many people "get the dates wrong".

This is only an estimate, though. To confirm whether you actually ovulated, you need basal body temperature, ovulation test strips or ultrasound monitoring (see How Do You Know If You're Ovulating? for how to take and read basal body temperature).

What Counts as a Warning Sign?

  • Cycles persistently shorter than 24 days or longer than 38 days
  • Fewer than 8 periods a year
  • Cycle length that swings widely from month to month
  • Bleeding that lasts more than 8 days
  • A sudden marked increase or decrease in flow
  • Bleeding between periods
  • Bleeding after sex
  • No period for three months or more (and not pregnant)
  • Period pain bad enough to take time off, or that painkillers don't touch

Of these, bleeding between periods and bleeding after sex deserve particular attention. Both should be checked and shouldn't be put down to "irregular periods".

How Does Chinese Medicine View the Menstrual Cycle?

Chinese medicine assesses a period on four aspects: timing, volume, colour and texture. As for the cycle itself, there are mainly three types:

TypeDefinitionCommon patterns
Early menstruationA cycle seven or more days earlyQi deficiency (qi failing to contain the blood), blood heat (heat forcing the blood to move recklessly)
Delayed menstruationA cycle seven or more days lateKidney deficiency, blood deficiency, blood cold, qi stagnation, phlegm-dampness
Irregular timingSometimes early, sometimes late, with no fixed patternLiver constraint, kidney deficiency

Besides the cycle, Chinese medicine also asks about:

  • Volume: heavy flow is mostly qi deficiency or blood heat; scanty flow is mostly blood deficiency, kidney deficiency or blood stasis
  • Colour: pale red is mostly deficiency; deep red is mostly heat; dark purple with clots is mostly stasis
  • Texture: thin and watery is mostly deficiency-cold; thick and sticky is mostly heat
  • The character of period pain: distending pain is mostly qi stagnation; stabbing pain is mostly blood stasis; dull pain that likes pressure is mostly deficiency

This is also why a Chinese medicine consultation asks so many detailed questions — it's the details taken together that form the basis of pattern differentiation.

The above is a general introduction to TCM theory, not a diagnosis or a prescription. Herbal treatment needs an in-person consultation and pattern differentiation by a registered Chinese medicine practitioner.

How to Keep a Record of Your Cycle

Whether you use a phone app or paper, we suggest recording for at least three months, and including the following:

  • The first and last day of each bleed
  • Daily amount of bleeding (estimated by how often you change protection is fine)
  • Period pain level (0 to 10)
  • Changes in discharge
  • Any marked mood or physical symptoms

The value of a record lies in the quality of information at your follow-up. "It doesn't seem very regular" and "over the last six months it's been 27, 31, 26, 38, 29 and 33 days" give the doctor completely different things to work with.

If you are trying to conceive, recording basal body temperature as well gives you more information (see How Do You Know If You're Ovulating?).

When to See a Doctor

  • Any of the warning signs above
  • No period for three months or more and not pregnant
  • Persistently irregular cycles, especially with excess hair growth, acne or weight gain (see PMOS (Formerly PCOS))
  • Hot flushes, night sweats or markedly lighter periods before the age of 40 (see Does a Low AMH Mean You Can't Get Pregnant?)
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35) (see Why Can't I Get Pregnant?)
  • Period pain that gets worse over time, or pain during sex (endometriosis needs to be ruled out)

Frequently Asked Questions

Is a 35-day cycle abnormal?

By the FIGO standard (24 to 38 days) it is within the normal range. What matters is whether it is stable — if it's about 35 days every month, that usually means a longer follicular phase but still regular ovulation. It's when the length keeps swinging that it needs more attention.

Do irregular periods always mean a hormone problem?

Not necessarily. Stress, lack of sleep, a marked change in weight, excessive exercise, travel and changes of routine can all alter a cycle temporarily. But if it stays irregular for more than three months, you should be checked; common causes include PMOS (formerly known as PCOS), thyroid disorders and hyperprolactinaemia.

How can I tell whether I'm ovulating?

The more reliable methods include a biphasic basal body temperature chart, an LH surge on ovulation test strips, ultrasound monitoring of the follicles, and a mid-luteal progesterone blood test. Inferring it from a regular cycle alone isn't enough — some women have regular cycles but still don't ovulate.

Are clots in period blood normal?

Small clots (about the size of a coin or smaller) are common on heavier days. But if large clots keep appearing, or come with a marked increase in flow, dizziness and tiredness, you should be checked for fibroids, adenomyosis or anaemia.

Does the "period" I get while on the contraceptive pill count?

The bleeding you get on the combined pill is a withdrawal bleed, which isn't the same as a natural period and doesn't reflect whether you are really ovulating. After stopping the pill, your own cycle takes a while to return, and only then can your original pattern be assessed.

Can I see a Chinese medicine practitioner for irregular periods?

Yes, but we suggest having basic tests first to rule out thyroid problems, high prolactin and PMOS, and then deciding the direction of care. Treatment once the cause is clear is far more effective than treatment based on guesswork.

Conclusion

Your menstrual cycle is one of the easiest and most honest indicators of how your body is doing. The criteria aren't complicated: 24 to 38 days, bleeding within 8 days, and not much difference from month to month.

Rather than agonising over one month's number, build the habit of keeping a record. Three months of complete records beat ten descriptions of "it doesn't seem very regular" — whether you see a Western doctor or a Chinese medicine practitioner, it's the most useful piece of information you can bring.
