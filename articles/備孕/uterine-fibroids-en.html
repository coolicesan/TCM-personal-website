Do Uterine Fibroids Need to Be Removed?Position Matters More Than Size

Uterine fibroids are very common, and most cause no symptoms and need no treatment. What matters is not size but position. A Hong Kong registered Chinese medicine practitioner explains the difference between the three positions, when treatment is needed, the real effect on fertility, and what Chinese medicine can and cannot do.

At a glanceUterine fibroids are the most common benign tumours in women of reproductive age — by the age of fifty, as many as seven or eight in ten women may have them — but most cause no symptoms and need no treatment. What really decides whether to act is not "how many centimetres" but the position, the symptoms and your plans for having a family. The chance of malignant change is less than one in a hundred.

"The scan shows a 4 cm fibroid" — after hearing that, the most common reaction is to search online for "how big before surgery". But that is the wrong question to ask.

Two fibroids of the same 4 cm can have completely different effects depending on where they sit: some cause heavy bleeding and anaemia every month, while others give no sign for ten years. Here is where the differences lie.

What Are Uterine Fibroids?

Uterine fibroids (leiomyomas)
Benign tumours formed by the overgrowth of smooth muscle cells in the uterus. They respond to oestrogen, so they tend to grow during the reproductive years and shrink after the menopause.

A few basic facts:

  • Very common: by the age of fifty, statistics for different populations put the proportion at roughly seven to eight in ten
  • Usually multiple: there is generally more than one
  • Usually no symptoms: many women never know they have them
  • Malignant change is rare: less than one in a hundred
  • Most shrink after the menopause

In other words, "having fibroids" is not, in itself, a medical finding to panic about.

Position Matters More Than Size

This is the one point from the whole article most worth remembering. The wall of the uterus has three layers, and fibroids fall into three main types according to where they grow:

TypePositionTypical effectsEffect on fertility
Submucosal fibroidBulges into the uterine cavityMuch heavier flow, prolonged periods, anaemiaClearest effect
Intramural fibroidGrows within the muscle layer of the uterusDepends on size and whether it presses on the cavityAffects fertility if it presses on and distorts the cavity; otherwise still debated
Subserosal fibroidBulges out from the outer wall of the uterusSense of pressure, frequent urination, constipation, lower back discomfortGenerally little effect

There is also a more detailed FIGO classification internationally (types 0 to 8), which you may see on a doctor's report — the lower the number, the closer the fibroid is to or the further into the uterine cavity.

What this means in practice: a 2 cm submucosal fibroid can be far more troublesome than a 6 cm subserosal one. So when you read a report, look at the position first, then the size.

Common Symptoms

  • Markedly heavier flow, large clots, prolonged periods
  • Anaemia from heavy blood loss (dizziness, tiredness, pale complexion, breathlessness)
  • A sense of pressure in the lower abdomen, a swollen abdomen
  • Frequent urination, or a feeling of not emptying the bladder fully
  • Constipation, discomfort opening your bowels
  • Lower back ache
  • Discomfort during sex
  • Difficulty conceiving or recurrent miscarriage

Heavier flow is the most common reason for seeking help, and also the one most easily overlooked — many women have spent years thinking "my periods have always been heavy".

When Is Treatment Needed?

In most cases, it isn't. If there are no symptoms, no effect on your plans for a family and no sign of rapid growth, regular follow-up and observation is enough.

Situations that do need attention:

  • Heavy flow leading to anaemia
  • Pressure symptoms affecting daily life (severe frequent urination, constipation, bloating)
  • Marked pain
  • An effect on fertility, or a link with recurrent miscarriage
  • Marked growth over a short time — needs further assessment
  • Continued growth after the menopause — this needs to be checked promptly

Diagnostic Tests

If you have heavy periods, remember to ask for your haemoglobin to be checked — anaemia from long-term blood loss is quite common, and it brings tiredness, hair loss and palpitations along with it.

Treatment Options

The direction depends on symptoms, position, age and plans for a family.

Observation and follow-up: the standard approach for those without symptoms, with a repeat ultrasound at regular intervals.

Medication: mainly to control bleeding and symptoms, for example anti-bleeding (haemostatic) medicines, non-steroidal anti-inflammatory painkillers, and hormonal preparations (an intrauterine system containing progestogen, the oral contraceptive pill, GnRH analogues and so on). Be aware: medication generally can't remove fibroids, symptoms may return when it is stopped, and some drugs are not suitable if you want to conceive while taking them.

Surgery — myomectomy: keeps the uterus, and suits those who plan to have children. Submucosal fibroids can often be dealt with through a hysteroscope. It's worth knowing that fibroids can recur after surgery: the literature records about 15% to a third, and some women need another operation later.

Surgery — hysterectomy: the definitive approach, suited to those who have completed their family, have severe symptoms and for whom other methods have failed.

Uterine artery embolisation and focused ultrasound ablation: minimally invasive alternatives; their suitability for those who plan to conceive is still debated and needs individual assessment with your doctor.

The Link with Fertility

This section is worth setting out separately, because a common saying goes "if you have fibroids you'd better have them cut out before you try to get pregnant", and the reality is more complicated.

  • Submucosal fibroids: the negative effect on implantation and fertility is the clearest, and treatment is generally recommended.
  • Intramural fibroids that press on the cavity: most consider them worth treating.
  • Intramural fibroids that don't press on the cavity: still debated — especially smaller ones, there is no consistent conclusion on whether to remove them.
  • Subserosal fibroids: generally thought to have little effect on fertility.

It must be said honestly that even for submucosal fibroids, the research shows an improved chance of natural conception after removal, but no statistically significant difference in ongoing pregnancy or live birth rate. This means the benefit of surgery exists, but you shouldn't expect it to guarantee a result.

If you are trying to conceive and have fibroids, the most practical approach is to discuss the position, the size, your age and your overall test results together with your gynaecologist, rather than deciding on "whether you have fibroids" alone (see Why Can't I Get Pregnant?).

How Does Chinese Medicine View Uterine Fibroids?

Chinese medicine classes them under "zheng jia" (abdominal masses) — "zheng" referring to a palpable mass that is fixed and doesn't move. The core of the pathology is blood stasis binding internally, though the reasons stasis forms differ.

Patterns often overlap in practice. One thing to note in particular is that where heavy flow has led to deficiency of qi and blood, the prescription needs to both hold the blood in and support the body's healthy qi, rather than relying on attacking and dispelling alone.

The above is a general introduction to TCM theory, not a diagnosis or a prescription. Herbal treatment needs an in-person consultation and pattern differentiation by a registered Chinese medicine practitioner.

What Can Chinese Medicine Do?

What it can't do

  • It can't remove fibroids that have already formed. Be highly sceptical of any claim to "dissolve" or "shrink" fibroids.
  • It can't replace necessary surgery.
  • It can't deal with severe anaemia that has already developed — that needs medical treatment such as iron replacement, or even a blood transfusion.

Where there is room to help

  • Regulating flow and period pain
  • Anaemia-related fatigue, dizziness and poor complexion (alongside iron replacement from Western medicine)
  • Mood, sleep and overall condition
  • Recovery of strength after surgery

The state of the evidence: research on Chinese medicine for uterine fibroids is mostly small trials, mostly using symptom improvement as the measure, with no large, high-quality randomised controlled studies.

Changes in the size of a fibroid should be followed up with ultrasound, not judged by symptom improvement or by feel. If your symptoms improve during Chinese medicine care but you haven't been rescanned, you should still have regular ultrasounds as your doctor advises.

When to See a Doctor

  • Markedly heavier flow, prolonged periods, large clots
  • Symptoms of anaemia: dizziness, tiredness, breathlessness, palpitations, pale complexion
  • A sense of pressure in the lower abdomen, a growing waistline
  • Severe frequent urination, difficulty passing urine, or constipation
  • A fibroid that grows markedly in a short time
  • A fibroid that keeps growing after the menopause — get it checked promptly
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35)
  • Sudden severe abdominal pain — seek care immediately, to rule out degeneration or torsion of a fibroid

Frequently Asked Questions

How big does a fibroid have to be before surgery?

There is no absolute size cut-off. The deciding factors are how severe the symptoms are, the position, plans for a family and the rate of growth. A small submucosal fibroid may need treating, while a larger subserosal fibroid without symptoms may not.

Can a fibroid turn into cancer?

The chance is less than one in a hundred. But if a fibroid grows rapidly over a short time, or keeps growing after the menopause, it should be checked promptly — both situations need further assessment.

Can I get pregnant with fibroids?

In most cases, yes. The clearest effect is from submucosal fibroids; subserosal fibroids generally have little effect; and for intramural fibroids it depends on whether they press on the cavity. Your gynaecologist should assess this for your own situation.

Will fibroids come back after they're removed?

They may. The recurrence rate after myomectomy is recorded in the literature at about 15% to a third, and some women need further treatment later. That is one of the factors to weigh when choosing the type of surgery.

Can herbal medicine shrink fibroids?

The existing evidence doesn't support that claim. A more reasonable role for Chinese medicine is regulating flow and period pain, and helping with anaemia-related fatigue and your general state. Be highly sceptical of any course that promises to "dissolve" fibroids, and follow fibroid size with ultrasound.

Will eating soy products or soya milk make fibroids grow?

This concern is common, but there is currently no sound evidence that soy products in ordinary dietary amounts make fibroids grow. If you have a specific worry, discuss it with your doctor or a registered dietitian; there's no need to cut them out altogether.

Will they shrink by themselves after the menopause?

Most do. Fibroids respond to oestrogen, so when oestrogen falls after the menopause they tend to shrink and symptoms mostly improve. That is why, for women near the menopause with mild symptoms, observation is sometimes chosen.

Conclusion

When fibroids are found, the first thing to do isn't to work out the size but to ask three questions: where is it? Is it causing symptoms? Do I plan to have a family?

Most fibroids need no treatment, only regular follow-up. For those that do need attention, there is a range of options from medication to surgery, and surgery isn't needed as soon as one is found.

Chinese medicine can help with flow, period pain, anaemia-related fatigue and your general state, but it can't remove fibroids — that has to be said clearly, because claims to the contrary are not uncommon on the market.
