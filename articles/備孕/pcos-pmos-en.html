At a glanceIn May 2026, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) was officially renamed PMOS (polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome). The change was made because the old name led people to think the problem was cysts on the ovaries, when in fact this is a multi-system condition involving the endocrine system, metabolism, reproduction, skin and mood. There is a three-year transition period during which both names will be used side by side.

"The scan says my ovaries are full of little cysts" — that sentence has left countless women believing their ovaries are covered in cysts. In fact this is the biggest misunderstanding the old name caused, and the direct reason for the international renaming. Below we start with the renaming, then cover symptoms, diagnosis and the direction of care.

The 2026 Renaming: From PCOS to PMOS

On 12 May 2026, a multistep global consensus study published in The Lancet officially renamed polycystic ovary syndrome as polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS).

Three reasons for the change

  • "Cyst" is misleading. What shows on the ultrasound is not a pathological cyst but small follicles whose development has stalled. Related research also indicates that this condition does not increase the rate of abnormal ovarian cysts.
  • The old name hid the core problem. This is a multi-system condition centred on the endocrine system and metabolism, and its effects go well beyond the ovaries.
  • Delayed diagnosis and stigma. The name led people to focus on gynaecology and fertility, so the metabolic and emotional problems of many patients were overlooked for years.

What it means for you in practice: for now the two names will run in parallel. Your doctor may still say PCOS, and reports may still use the old name; this is a normal part of the transition. The diagnostic criteria and management have not changed because of the rename.

What Is PMOS?

PMOS (formerly PCOS)
A long-term condition centred on endocrine and metabolic imbalance, characterised by disrupted ovulation, raised androgen levels, and ovaries with a multifollicular appearance on ultrasound. It affects about 1 in 8 women worldwide.

It is not a disease of a single organ but a set of interlinked problems: insulin resistance affects androgen levels, androgens affect ovulation, irregular ovulation affects the periods and the uterine lining, while weight, mood and sleep in turn affect overall metabolism.

Common Symptoms

Not everyone gets every symptom, and the picture varies widely:

Periods and reproduction

  • Longer cycles, infrequent periods, or periods that stay away for a long time
  • Irregular or absent ovulation
  • Difficulty working out when you ovulate

Androgen-related

  • Excess hair growth on the face, chin, chest and abdomen
  • Persistent acne, especially on the chin and along the jawline
  • Thinning hair on the crown of the head

Metabolic

  • Weight gain, or difficulty losing weight
  • Darkened skin on the back of the neck, in the armpits and similar places (acanthosis nigricans)
  • Abnormal blood sugar or blood lipids

Mood and other

  • Higher rates of anxiety and low mood than in the general population
  • Poor sleep and daytime tiredness

How Is It Diagnosed?

Diagnosis can't rest on an ultrasound report alone. The approach generally used internationally is that, after other possible causes have been ruled out, two of the following three features are present:

Diagnostic featureDescription
Disrupted ovulationInfrequent periods, irregular cycles or no ovulation
Signs of high androgensClinical features (excess hair, acne, hair loss) or blood tests showing raised androgens
Ovarian morphologyUltrasound showing a multifollicular appearance, or a raised anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) level

A few points are worth noting:

  • The criteria are different for teenagers, because irregular periods and acne are common in adolescence anyway, and the label shouldn't be applied too early.
  • Other conditions must be ruled out first, including thyroid disorders, hyperprolactinaemia and congenital adrenal hyperplasia.
  • Ultrasound alone can't confirm the diagnosis. Many women without the condition also show a multifollicular appearance on ultrasound.

If all you've been told is "you have lots of cysts on your ovaries" and you haven't had any other tests, it's worth asking your doctor for a full assessment.

More Than a Fertility Issue

This is the core message the renaming is meant to get across. The effects of PMOS are long-term and wide-ranging:

  • Metabolism: insulin resistance is common, and the risk of type 2 diabetes later in life is higher
  • Cardiovascular: blood lipids, blood pressure and long-term cardiovascular risk need monitoring
  • The uterine lining: long-term lack of ovulation means the lining doesn't shed cyclically, so the risk of endometrial hyperplasia needs watching
  • Mood: rates of anxiety and depression are markedly higher — the item most often overlooked
  • Sleep: rates of sleep-disordered breathing are higher

So even if you have no plans to conceive right now, regular monitoring of blood sugar, blood lipids, blood pressure and your periods still matters. Going months without a period isn't a perk; it's a signal that needs attention.

How Does Chinese Medicine View PMOS?

Chinese medicine has no disease name for PMOS (or for "polycystic ovaries"); the related presentations mostly fall under delayed menstruation, amenorrhoea, infertility and abdominal masses (zheng jia). The core of the pathology is usually linked to the kidney, spleen and liver, and the following patterns are commonly seen in practice.

Kidney deficiencyDelayed periods, scanty flow or amenorrhoeaAching lower back and knees, aversion to cold, frequent night-time urination, reduced libidoTonify the kidney and replenish essence
Phlegm-dampnessInfrequent periods, sluggish flowHeavier build, chest tightness with phlegm, heavy head, abundant thick white dischargeResolve phlegm and dispel dampness
Liver constraintIrregular cycles, pre-period distension and painLow mood or irritability, tight, full chest and ribs, insomniaSoothe the liver and regulate qi

A single pattern is rarely seen in practice; they usually overlap — for example kidney deficiency with phlegm-dampness, or liver constraint with blood stasis. This is also why two people with the same PMOS diagnosis may be treated in completely different directions by Chinese medicine.

The above is a general introduction to TCM theory, not a diagnosis or a prescription. Herbal treatment needs an in-person consultation and pattern differentiation by a registered Chinese medicine practitioner, and shouldn't be self-prescribed.

How Does Chinese Medicine Approach It?

Treating by phase of the cycle

This is the most distinctive way Chinese medicine handles period-related problems: instead of using one formula for the whole course, the emphasis of the prescription shifts with the four phases of the menstrual cycle.

PhaseApproximate timingWhat the body is doingFocus of treatment
MenstruationDays 1–5 of bleedingThe uterus is open and the menstrual blood is dischargedInvigorate blood and regulate menstruation, working with the body's natural flow
Follicular phaseAfter the period until before ovulationYin and blood gradually build, follicles developNourish the kidney and blood; replenish yin essence
OvulationMid-cycleThe transition from yin to yangInvigorate blood and unblock the channels to promote the transition
Luteal phaseAfter ovulation until before the periodYang growingWarm kidney yang, while also soothing the liver

The difficulty with this approach in PMOS is that many patients don't ovulate regularly at all, so the cycle is hard to divide into phases. In practice it is usually necessary to first establish what is actually happening with basal body temperature, ultrasound monitoring or hormone tests, and only then decide how to divide the phases — which is also why you can't copy somebody else's prescription.

Herbal direction by pattern

  • Kidney deficiency: tonifying the kidney is the main thread, often further divided into the yang-deficient or yin-deficient type.
  • Phlegm-dampness: strengthen the spleen and resolve phlegm and dampness; this type often needs a longer course, closely tied to lifestyle management.
  • Liver constraint: soothing the liver and regulating qi comes first, and improvements in sleep and mood often show up before those in the periods.
  • Blood stasis: invigorate blood and resolve stasis; this usually appears together with other patterns.

Acupuncture

Points are generally chosen from the Ren (conception) vessel and the kidney, spleen and stomach channels, and adjusted by pattern. In practice it is more often used to regulate the regularity of periods and to improve mood and sleep.

Course of treatment and expectations

PMOS is a long-term condition, and care is measured in months, not weeks. You generally need to observe at least three menstrual cycles to judge whether the direction is right; if you had no periods for a long time to begin with, the assessment may take longer.

Be sceptical of any claim to restore ovulation within a short time or to guarantee a particular result.

Working with lifestyle

This part isn't an extra but the core. International guidelines are unanimous in making lifestyle management first-line, and the daily-living guidance in Chinese medicine points the same way — regular routines, moderate exercise, less refined sugar and processed food, and attention to sleep and stress.

However appropriate the prescription, if sleep and diet aren't adjusted the effect is usually limited. That is worth saying frankly.

What Does the Research Say?

PMOS is one of the areas with more evidence in Chinese medicine research, but the results are not uniformly positive. Presenting them honestly is worth more than citing them selectively.

Acupuncture: the largest trial so far was negative

The PCOSAct study, published in the Journal of the American Medical Association (JAMA) in 2017, is the largest randomised controlled trial in this area — it recruited 1,000 women with PMOS (then called PCOS) at 21 sites (27 hospitals) in mainland China, with live birth rate as the main outcome.

The results:

GroupLive birth rate
Real acupuncture + clomiphene29.4%
Sham acupuncture + clomiphene28.0%
Real acupuncture + placebo13.9%
Sham acupuncture + placebo16.8%

In other words, acupuncture did not raise the live birth rate; what made the real difference was the drug. After publication the study prompted a good deal of discussion in the Chinese medicine community, and some scholars raised questions about the trial design and the needling protocol, but on the measure of live birth the conclusion is quite clear.

Herbal medicine and berberine

Berberine (huang lian su) is the main active compound of herbs such as coptis root (huang lian) and phellodendron bark (huang bai), and is the single compound most studied in this area.

  • A meta-analysis of 9 randomised controlled trials (which also searched Chinese databases such as CNKI, Wanfang and VIP) showed that berberine was not significantly different from metformin in improving insulin resistance, glucose and lipid metabolism and reproductive endocrine markers; but the authors also noted that the existing data are not sufficient to draw firm conclusions.
  • Another systematic review found that berberine's live birth rate was similar to that of placebo or metformin but lower than that of letrozole (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44–0.82), and concluded that there is no firm evidence that berberine improves live birth or other clinical outcomes.

As for compound herbal formulas, the Cochrane Collaboration has a dedicated review assessing the effect of Chinese herbal medicine in subfertile women with PMOS. The conclusion of the 2021 update is quite clear: the existing evidence is not sufficient to support the use of Chinese herbal medicine for these women. None of the included studies reported the live birth rate, and the data available for other outcomes were also very limited. The authors also pointed out that this area needs more rigorously designed, adequately followed-up, high-quality studies that use live birth rate as the outcome.

Note the scope of that review: it included only women hoping to conceive naturally and excluded cases receiving insemination or assisted reproductive technology, so its conclusions can't be applied directly to the situation of working alongside an IVF cycle.

A meta-analysis of animal experiments shows a positive effect of Chinese herbal medicine on ovarian weight, sex hormones and insulin resistance, but the authors state clearly that there is marked publication bias and that results from animal models can't be applied directly to the clinic.

An important reminder: berberine is a single compound and is not the same thing as a Chinese medicine prescription based on pattern differentiation. The studies above don't mean "taking coptis will do the job", and you shouldn't buy and take it on your own — it has its own indications and side effects, and may interact with other medicines.

How should these results be understood?

Taken together, the existing evidence points to a fairly clear positioning:

  • On directly raising the live birth rate: there is currently no reliable evidence that acupuncture or herbal medicine can do this, and the largest trial was negative.
  • On metabolic markers: there are some positive signals, especially berberine performing similarly to metformin on insulin resistance.
  • On regular periods, mood and sleep: this is where Chinese medicine practitioners more often see improvement in practice, and the part patients feel most directly, but high-quality controlled studies are still lacking.
  • Overall research quality: there is a fair amount of Chinese-language literature, but sample size, blinding and the consistency of outcome measures still have room to improve.

So the more realistic understanding is: the role of Chinese medicine in PMOS is as part of long-term management — working on metabolism, regular periods, sleep and mood — and not a shortcut to a higher pregnancy success rate. If you are trying to conceive, ovulation-inducing drugs and assisted reproductive technology have clear evidence behind them, and shouldn't be replaced or delayed by Chinese medicine care.

A word on expectations

PMOS is a long-term condition, and at present there is no way to "cure it for good". Whether in Chinese or Western medicine, the aims of management are to improve symptoms, restore regular periods, control metabolic risk and improve quality of life.

Chinese medicine care shouldn't replace necessary endocrine or gynaecological follow-up either; the two can run side by side. If you are having any treatment or preparing for an assisted reproduction cycle, make sure both practitioners know about each other.

When to See a Doctor

  • No period for three months or more
  • Cycles persistently longer than 35 days, or fewer than 8 periods a year
  • Marked excess hair growth, severe acne or thinning hair on the crown
  • Darkened, thickened skin on the back of the neck or in the armpits
  • A marked rise in weight over a short time
  • A family history of diabetes, high blood pressure or cardiovascular disease
  • Persistently low mood or anxiety that affects daily life
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35)

A particular reminder on the last point: even with a confirmed PMOS diagnosis, if you are having difficulty conceiving you should still have a full assessment, including tests for your partner — the problem isn't necessarily on one side only.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between PCOS and PMOS?

There is none; they are the old and new names of the same condition. It was officially renamed in May 2026, with a three-year transition period during which both names will be used side by side. The diagnostic criteria and management haven't changed.

Do cysts on the ovaries definitely mean PMOS?

No. The "cysts" seen on ultrasound are small follicles whose development has stalled, not pathological cysts, and many women without the condition see them too. Diagnosis needs two of the three criteria to be met, with other causes ruled out; ultrasound alone can't confirm it.

Can PMOS be cured for good?

There is currently no cure. It is a long-term condition, and the aim of management is to control symptoms and long-term health risks. With lifestyle management and appropriate treatment, many women are able to restore regular periods and improve their metabolic markers.

If my periods don't come, can I ignore it?

It needs attention. Long-term lack of ovulation means the uterine lining doesn't shed cyclically, which carries a long-term risk of endometrial hyperplasia. Also, missing periods often reflect an underlying endocrine and metabolic problem that hasn't been dealt with.

Does losing weight really help?

International guidelines make lifestyle management first-line, and weight management helps improve ovulation and metabolic markers. But a caution: crash dieting or extreme weight loss can backfire and affect your periods and mood. We suggest making changes gradually, under professional guidance.

Can Chinese medicine be combined with Western treatment?

Yes, but both practitioners must know about all the treatments you are having and every medication you take — especially while you are using ovulation-inducing drugs or preparing for an assisted reproduction cycle.

Do I need to manage it even if I'm not planning a pregnancy?

Yes. The effects of PMOS aren't limited to fertility; they include blood sugar, blood lipids, blood pressure, the uterine lining and mental health. Even if you have no plans to conceive for now, you should still monitor the relevant markers regularly.

Conclusion

The point of this renaming is to pull the focus back from "cysts on the ovaries" to the real core — a long-term imbalance of the endocrine system and metabolism. For patients it means that management shouldn't stop at regulating periods or trying to conceive, but also take in blood sugar, blood lipids, weight, sleep and mood.

Chinese medicine can work on this by differentiating patterns — kidney deficiency, phlegm-dampness, liver constraint, blood stasis — and prescribing by phase of the cycle, while also addressing sleep and mood, the background factors that affect overall metabolism. But this is long-term management, not short-term treatment, and should run alongside follow-up with an endocrinologist or gynaecologist, not replace it.
