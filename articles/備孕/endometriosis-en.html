At a glanceEndometriosis is when tissue like the lining of the uterus grows outside the uterine cavity, bleeds with each cycle but has no way out, and causes inflammation and adhesions; when it grows on the ovary, the cyst it forms is what's commonly called a "chocolate cyst". It is estimated to affect about one in ten women of reproductive age, and the average delay from first symptoms to diagnosis is 4 to 11 years. The good news is that since 2022, international guidelines have moved to imaging first, so surgery is no longer needed before treatment can begin.

"The scan shows a chocolate cyst" — many women find out by chance while being checked for something else, and many others have had pain for ten years before they learn the cause. This article explains what it is, what the clues are, and how it is diagnosed and managed today.

What Is It?

Endometriosis
Tissue resembling the lining of the uterus grows in places beyond the uterine cavity and muscle wall, such as the ovaries, the pelvic peritoneum, the bowel or the surface of the bladder, usually accompanied by chronic inflammation.

This tissue responds to the menstrual cycle and bleeds in the same way, but unlike normal menstrual blood it cannot leave the body, so it builds up where it is, causing inflammation, pain and adhesions.

A chocolate cyst (medically, an ovarian endometrioma) is the cyst that forms when this process happens in the ovary — it is named because the old blood that collects is dark brown and thick, like melted chocolate. It is not cancer, but it can affect ovarian tissue and fertility.

The two most telling clues are pain during sex and pain when opening your bowels during your period — neither is typical of ordinary period pain, but many women don't mention them to their doctor, either from embarrassment or because they assume they have nothing to do with their period.

An important point: the severity of symptoms doesn't necessarily match the extent of the disease. Some women have widespread lesions and barely notice them, while others have mild-looking scans and are in too much pain to work. Your pain doesn't need "imaging evidence" before it counts.

Why Does It Take So Long to Be Diagnosed?

An average delay of 4 to 11 years has several layers to it:

  • Period pain is taken for granted — "period pain is normal for women" is the biggest obstacle
  • The symptoms vary — they may be put down to a digestive problem, cystitis or stress
  • Diagnosis used to depend on surgery — if confirming it meant an operation, the threshold was naturally high

The third point has changed in recent years.

How It's Diagnosed: What Changed After 2022

The European Society of Human Reproduction and Embryology (ESHRE) updated its guideline in 2022, and laparoscopy is no longer the gold standard for diagnosis. The order now is:

  • Clinical assessment — the pattern of symptoms and a pelvic examination
  • Imaging — mainly transvaginal ultrasound; MRI when deep lesions are suspected or the ultrasound is uncertain
  • Laparoscopy — recommended only when imaging is negative and empirical treatment hasn't worked or isn't suitable

Also, the CA-125 blood test is not suitable as a screening test — it has low sensitivity in the early stages and also rises in other conditions.

What this means in practice: if you have typical symptoms, you can start with an ultrasound and discuss a management plan with your doctor, and you don't need to have surgery before treatment can begin. This lowers the barrier to seeking help considerably.

Treatment Options

The direction depends on three things: how much the symptoms bother you, whether you plan to have a baby, and where the lesions are.

Pain relief: non-steroidal anti-inflammatory painkillers are the usual first line, and tend to work better if taken when the pain is just starting.

Hormonal treatment: this includes the combined oral contraceptive pill, progestogens, the intrauterine system and GnRH analogues, working by suppressing the cyclical activity of the lesions. Note: you cannot get pregnant while on hormonal treatment, so anyone planning a pregnancy needs to work out a separate timetable.

Surgery: used when medication hasn't worked, the lesions are affecting the function of an organ, or the chocolate cyst is large. Surgery may improve pain and some fertility outcomes, but surgery on an ovarian cyst can affect ovarian reserve, and this must be fully discussed with your doctor if you plan to conceive.

Assisted reproduction: if infertility is also present, IUI or IVF may need to be considered (see IVF Step by Step).

No single approach suits everyone — with the same diagnosis, the direction for a 25-year-old who wants to regulate her periods and a 38-year-old who wants to conceive can be completely different.

The Link with Infertility

Endometriosis is markedly more common among women with infertility. The possible mechanisms include pelvic adhesions affecting tubal function, effects on ovarian tissue, and a chronic inflammatory environment.

But to be clear: a diagnosis doesn't mean you can't get pregnant. Quite a few women conceive naturally. If you haven't conceived after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35), you should have a full assessment (see Why Can't I Get Pregnant?).

A point about planning: if you have a confirmed diagnosis and plan to have a baby, it's worth discussing the overall timetable with your doctor early — because you can't conceive while on hormonal treatment, and there is also a trade-off between surgery and ovarian reserve.

How Does Chinese Medicine View It?

Chinese medicine has no term for this condition. The related presentations mostly fall under painful periods, abdominal masses (zheng jia) and infertility. The core of the pathology is blood stasis, though the reasons stasis forms differ.

What Can Chinese Medicine Do?

What it can't do

  • It can't remove lesions or cysts that have already formed
  • It can't replace necessary surgery or hormonal treatment
  • It can't clear fallopian tubes that have already adhered

Where there is room to help

  • Pain relief and managing general discomfort
  • Mood, sleep and fatigue — the burden in these areas is generally heavy for people with chronic pain
  • Regulating the menstrual cycle
  • Supporting your overall condition during Western medical treatment

The state of the evidence needs to be stated honestly: research on Chinese medicine for endometriosis is mostly small trials, with no large, high-quality randomised controlled studies, so the strength of the evidence is limited.

If you are diagnosed, Chinese medicine care should run alongside gynaecological follow-up, not instead of it — especially if you have a larger cyst, or plan to conceive and time is limited.

What You Can Do Day to Day

  • Apply heat to your lower abdomen or lower back
  • Keep up regular but not excessive activity
  • Manage sleep and stress — both genuinely affect how pain feels
  • Keep a record of pain scores and medication, so you can give accurate information at follow-up
  • Take painkillers when you need them — there's no need to wait until the pain is severe

When to See a Doctor

  • Period pain that makes you take time off, is getting worse year by year, or that painkillers help less and less
  • Pain during sex
  • Pain when opening your bowels or passing urine during your period
  • Persistent pelvic pain outside your period
  • A known ovarian cyst that has never been followed up
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35)
  • Sudden severe abdominal pain, fever or vomiting — seek care immediately to rule out a ruptured cyst or ovarian torsion

Frequently Asked Questions

Can a chocolate cyst turn into cancer?

The vast majority are benign. Malignant change is rare, but once it is diagnosed you should still follow your doctor's advice on regular follow-up, especially if the cyst is large or its appearance on imaging changes.

Do I definitely need surgery?

Not necessarily. Whether to operate depends on how severe the symptoms are, the size and features of the cyst, your plans for having a family and how you respond to medication. In particular, surgery on an ovarian cyst may affect ovarian reserve, so if you plan to conceive this must be fully discussed with your doctor.

Will it clear up by itself after I've had a baby?

Not necessarily. Symptoms may ease temporarily during pregnancy and breastfeeding because of hormonal changes, but the condition can still flare up again before menopause. It is something to manage over the long term, not a problem solved once and for all.

Does taking the contraceptive pill count as "treatment"?

Yes. Hormonal treatment is one of the standard approaches internationally; it works by suppressing the cyclical activity of the lesions and the pain that goes with it. But it can't remove lesions that already exist, symptoms may return when you stop, and you can't conceive while taking it.

Can Chinese medicine shrink a chocolate cyst?

The current evidence doesn't support that claim. A more reasonable role for Chinese medicine is relieving pain, helping with mood and sleep, and general care, alongside gynaecological follow-up. Changes in a cyst should be followed up with imaging, not judged by how you feel.

If the pain isn't severe, can I ignore it?

Mild symptoms don't mean follow-up isn't needed, because the severity of symptoms doesn't necessarily match the extent of the disease. If you have the typical clues (pain during sex, pain opening your bowels during your period, period pain that worsens year by year), it's worth having a check.

Conclusion

The biggest problem with endometriosis is not that it is hard to treat but that it is found too late — the average delay is measured in years, and the main reason is the idea that period pain is normal.

The way it's diagnosed has now changed: imaging first, with no need to operate first. If you have period pain that gets worse year by year, pain during sex or pain opening your bowels during your period, it's worth having a check.

There is no standard answer on management; it has to be decided individually, based on symptoms, plans for having a family and where the lesions are. Chinese medicine can support pain relief, mood and sleep and general care, but should run alongside gynaecological follow-up.
