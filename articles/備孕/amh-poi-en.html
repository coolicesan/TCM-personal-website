At a glanceAMH reflects roughly how many follicles remain in the ovaries, and it cannot predict your chance of conceiving naturally. International guidelines are explicit that ovarian reserve markers should not be used to predict natural pregnancy, should not be used as a reason to refuse assisted reproduction, and cannot predict the age of menopause. A low AMH means you may respond less strongly to ovarian stimulation drugs, but it does not mean you can't get pregnant.

"My doctor says my AMH is only 0.8 — does that mean I can't have a baby?" This is the most common question after the blood test, and the one that most often floors people. But the number is routinely misread. It does carry clinical meaning, just not where most people think. Here is what it does and doesn't tell you.

What Is AMH, and What Does It Reflect?

Anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH)
Secreted by the granulosa cells of the small follicles in the ovary. The level in the blood broadly reflects how many follicles remain. It can be checked with a blood test and is not affected by where you are in your menstrual cycle.

To understand AMH, start by separating two ideas:

ConceptWhat it meansIs AMH related?
Egg quantityHow many follicles are left in the ovariesYes — this is exactly what AMH reflects
Egg qualityWhether the eggs' chromosomes are normalNo — quality is mainly tied to age

This distinction is the key to everything else. Age determines quality; AMH reflects quantity, and when it comes to whether you get pregnant, quality often matters more. It also explains why a 30-year-old with a low AMH can still have a better chance of conceiving naturally than a 42-year-old with a normal AMH.

Does a Low AMH Mean You Can't Conceive?

No. This is the most widespread misunderstanding at present, and the position of the international professional bodies is clear.

In its committee opinion on ovarian reserve testing, the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) states that ovarian reserve markers cannot predict the current reproductive potential of women with no established fertility problem, and that their results should not be used to predict the chance of natural pregnancy.

Recent research supports this further. A study comparing women with "unexplained infertility" and women whose infertility was due to a male factor found no difference in AMH, antral follicle count or basal FSH between the two groups; if ovarian reserve really affected natural conception, the first group would be expected to score lower. The authors concluded that doctors and patients should both know this, to avoid inappropriate counselling and unnecessary clinical decisions.

Where AMH is genuinely useful is in predicting how you will respond to ovarian stimulation drugs during in vitro fertilisation (IVF) — with a smaller reserve, fewer eggs are usually retrieved. That is information for planning treatment, not a verdict on your fertility.

Three Things AMH Should Not Be Used For

Under the current international consensus:

  • It should not be used to predict natural fertility. This is the most commonly misused application.
  • It should not be used as a reason to refuse assisted reproduction. ASRM states explicitly that a very low AMH should not be grounds for refusing IVF treatment.
  • It should not be used to predict the age of menopause. AMH falls with age, but it cannot accurately work out when an individual will reach menopause.

Nor is it recommended that women without symptoms use ovarian reserve testing as a routine screen — the result tends to cause anxiety without answering the question she actually wants answered.

What Is Premature Ovarian Insufficiency (POI)?

Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI)
Also called primary ovarian insufficiency. It means the ovaries lose normal function before the age of 40, leading to periods stopping or becoming infrequent, falling oestrogen and reduced fertility.

Note the change in wording: the older term "premature ovarian failure" has gradually been replaced by POI, because "failure" is not accurate — ovarian function in POI can return intermittently, and some women still have occasional follicle development, and may even conceive naturally.

Common symptoms

  • Periods stopping or becoming markedly infrequent before 40
  • Hot flushes and night sweats
  • Vaginal dryness and discomfort during sex
  • Poorer sleep
  • Mood swings and difficulty concentrating
  • Difficulty conceiving

How it is diagnosed

Diagnosis rests mainly on periods that become infrequent or stop for several months before the age of 40, together with an elevated FSH level confirmed on repeat testing some time apart. The ASRM and ESHRE guideline states that AMH should not be the main diagnostic test for POI, although it can help confirm the diagnosis when it is in doubt.

Common causes

  • Chromosomal or genetic factors (for example Turner syndrome or fragile X premutation)
  • Autoimmune factors
  • Medical causes (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, ovarian surgery)
  • Infection
  • Most cases still have no known cause

If POI is confirmed, chromosome and related gene testing is generally recommended, as the results can affect risk assessment for other family members.

POI Is More Than a Fertility Issue

This point is extremely important, yet often overlooked. Long-term oestrogen deficiency affects:

  • Bone health: faster bone loss and a higher risk of osteoporosis
  • Cardiovascular health: an increased risk of cardiovascular disease
  • The urinary and genital tract: vaginal dryness and recurrent urinary discomfort
  • Cognition and mood: sleep, concentration and emotional wellbeing
  • Overall quality of life

So even if you have no plans to conceive, women with POI should still discuss long-term hormone replacement with their doctor — the aim is not only to ease hot flushes but to protect the bones and the heart. This falls within Western medical care, and Chinese medicine should not replace it.

How Does Chinese Medicine View Low AMH and Premature Ovarian Insufficiency?

Chinese medicine has no terms for AMH or POI. The related presentations are mostly classed under delayed menstruation, amenorrhoea, "blood exhaustion" and "periods ending before old age". The core of the pathology lies in the kidney, with links to the liver, spleen and heart.

In practice, patterns often overlap, and they frequently run alongside long-term stress, lack of sleep, extreme dieting or excessive exercise. These background factors are themselves recognised by modern medicine as affecting the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis.

The above is a general introduction to TCM theory, not a diagnosis or a prescription. Herbal treatment needs an in-person consultation and pattern differentiation by a registered Chinese medicine practitioner, and shouldn't be self-prescribed.

What Can Chinese Medicine Do, and What Can't It Do?

One point has to be made clear first: at present no method — Chinese medicine, Western medicine or supplements — has been proven to increase the number of follicles in the ovaries. The number of follicles is set at birth and declines with age, an irreversible physiological process. Any claim that you can "grow back more eggs" or "bring your AMH back up" should be treated with great scepticism.

It's also worth noting that AMH values fluctuate to some degree, and different assay methods give different results, so a single higher reading doesn't necessarily mean that ovarian reserve has actually increased.

Where Chinese medicine can reasonably help:

  • Easing symptoms such as hot flushes, night sweats, insomnia and mood swings
  • Working on lifestyle factors linked to the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis: stress, sleep, extreme dieting or exercise
  • Regulating overall qi, blood and energy levels
  • For those diagnosed with POI, supporting long-term health management alongside Western medicine

On the existing research: studies of Chinese medicine for POI or reduced ovarian reserve are currently mostly small trials, with no large randomised controlled trials using live birth rate as the main outcome, so the strength of the evidence is limited.

If you are trying to conceive and considering assisted reproduction, Chinese medicine care should not replace or delay the relevant assessment and treatment — especially with a low AMH, where time itself is a factor to consider.

When to See a Doctor

  • No period for four months or more before the age of 40
  • Markedly infrequent periods, with cycles getting longer and longer
  • Signs of low oestrogen such as hot flushes, night sweats or vaginal dryness
  • A family history of premature ovarian failure or early menopause
  • Previous chemotherapy, radiotherapy or ovarian surgery
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're over 35)
  • A confirmed diagnosis of POI without ever having discussed long-term hormone replacement

A particular reminder on the last point: after a diagnosis of POI, follow-up is not only about fertility but also about long-term protection of the bones and the heart.

Frequently Asked Questions

What AMH level is normal?

Reference ranges differ between laboratories and assay methods, and the value also has to be read against age — the same number means something different at 35 than at 25. Looking at the figure without considering age and clinical context makes it easy to reach the wrong conclusion. Ask your doctor to interpret it in light of your overall situation.

Can I conceive naturally with a low AMH?

Yes. The current evidence shows no clear link between ovarian reserve markers and the chance of natural conception. What AMH mainly predicts is the response to ovarian stimulation drugs, not whether you can get pregnant.

Is there any way to raise my AMH?

No method has been proven to genuinely increase the number of follicles. AMH naturally fluctuates, and different assay methods give different results, so an occasional higher reading doesn't necessarily reflect a real change. Be cautious about treatments or supplements that claim to raise AMH.

What's the difference between premature ovarian insufficiency and menopause?

Both the timing and the nature are different. POI occurs before the age of 40, and ovarian function can return intermittently, so ovulation is still possible; natural menopause is the permanent end of ovarian function. This is also why the name changed from "ovarian failure" to "ovarian insufficiency".

Can I still conceive naturally after a POI diagnosis?

Yes, but the chance is not high. Women with POI may have intermittent follicle development and ovulation, and natural pregnancies are reported in the literature. So if you aren't planning a pregnancy for now, you still need to think about contraception — a point that is often overlooked.

Do I need to do IVF straight away if my AMH is low?

That needs to be assessed by a fertility specialist based on your age, overall health and plans for having a family. ASRM states clearly that a very low AMH should not be a reason to refuse IVF, but the actual chance of success and the treatment plan need to be discussed individually.

How long does Chinese medicine care take?

Think in months, not weeks — you generally need to observe at least three menstrual cycles to judge the direction. But as explained above, the aim of Chinese medicine is to improve symptoms and overall condition, not to raise the number of eggs, so expectations need to be set accurately.

Conclusion

AMH is a useful but often misread marker. It reflects the number of eggs, not their quality, and it does not predict the chance of natural conception — that is the consistent position of the international professional bodies. When you receive a low number, what you need most is a full clinical assessment and a correct interpretation, not panic.

If premature ovarian insufficiency is confirmed, management should cover both fertility planning and long-term health protection, and the latter is often overlooked. Chinese medicine can differentiate patterns — kidney yin deficiency, kidney yang deficiency, kidney deficiency with liver constraint, heart–kidney disharmony — to work on hot flushes, insomnia, mood and energy, alongside adjustments to lifestyle factors; but it cannot increase the number of follicles, and it should not replace follow-up with a fertility or endocrine specialist.

Receiving a diagnosis like this often hits harder emotionally than physically. If it feels overwhelming, seeking psychological support and a community of people going through the same thing matters as much as looking after the body.
