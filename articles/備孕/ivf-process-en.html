IVF Step by StepWhat Happens at Each Stage, from Initial Tests to Your Pregnancy Test

Thinking about IVF but not sure what the process involves? This guide compares IUI with IVF and walks through each step — tests, ovarian stimulation, egg retrieval, embryo culture and the pregnancy test — and covers whether PGT is worth doing, public and private options in Hong Kong, and the warning signs that need urgent medical attention.

At a glanceAn IVF cycle, counted from your first injection to the pregnancy test, usually takes about four to six weeks. It has eight steps: initial tests, ovarian stimulation, the trigger injection, egg retrieval, fertilisation and embryo culture in the lab, embryo transfer, luteal support, and finally the pregnancy test. The part that really needs frequent trips to the clinic is the 8 to 10 days of stimulation.

Once you've decided to go ahead with IVF, what's most unsettling is often not the treatment itself but the feeling of "I have no idea what's going to happen". A flood of drug names, appointments and jargon arrives, and hardly anyone walks you through it from start to finish.

This guide takes the whole process in the order it actually happens: what you do at each step, how long it takes, what your body feels like and what to prepare. By the end you should have a fair picture of what a full course of treatment looks like.

Will You Try IUI Before IVF?

Many people find that at their first appointment the doctor suggests not IVF but IUI (intrauterine insemination). The two are often discussed together, but they are very different levels of treatment.

What is IUI?

Intrauterine insemination (IUI)
Around the time of ovulation, a sample of washed, concentrated, motile sperm is placed directly into the uterine cavity through a fine catheter, sparing the sperm the journey through the cervix.

The process is much simpler than most people expect. It is usually combined with oral ovulation-stimulating medication (such as letrozole or clomiphene) and ultrasound monitoring of the follicles. Once a follicle is mature, a trigger injection is given, and the insemination is carried out at the clinic at a set time. The insemination itself takes only a few minutes, needs no anaesthesia and feels similar to a gynaecological examination; after a short rest you can go home.

An important precondition: IUI needs at least one open fallopian tube, and the male partner's motile sperm count after washing has to reach a certain level. If the tubes are blocked, or there is a serious sperm problem, the chance of success with IUI is very low, and IVF is usually recommended straight away.

When is it reasonable to try IUI first?

  • Unexplained infertility
  • Mild male-factor infertility (the motile sperm count after washing is still adequate)
  • Ovulation problems (these respond well to ovulation-stimulating medication)
  • Cervical factors, or difficulty with intercourse
  • Use of donor sperm

When do people usually go straight to IVF?

  • Both fallopian tubes blocked or badly damaged
  • Severe male-factor infertility (ICSI needed)
  • Moderate to severe endometriosis
  • Older age with clearly reduced ovarian reserve
  • Several IUI cycles already tried without success
  • Genetic testing of embryos is needed (see the PGT section below)

How many IUI cycles should you try before moving to IVF?

The American Society for Reproductive Medicine's guideline on unexplained infertility recommends three to four cycles of "oral ovulation-stimulating medication plus IUI" as first-line treatment, and only then considering IVF. Clinical experience agrees: most IUI successes happen in the first three or four cycles, and each additional cycle adds only a limited amount of extra chance.

But age changes the sums. For people over 35, and especially those approaching 40, time itself is the critical variable. Many doctors will suggest fewer IUI cycles, or going straight to IVF, so that limited time isn't spent on a method with a lower success rate.

This is a decision to discuss with your doctor in light of your age, diagnosis and ovarian reserve. There is no single answer.

The Process Step by Step

Each step below covers just three things: what you do, how long it takes and what it feels like.

1. Initial tests

  • What you do: the female partner has blood tests for hormones and an ultrasound of the ovaries and uterus; the male partner has a semen analysis; both are screened for infectious diseases.
  • What it decides: the stimulation protocol, the drug doses, and whether ICSI or PGT is needed.
  • Key point: the male partner's tests matter just as much. About a third of infertility is linked to the male partner, so it shouldn't fall to the woman alone to be tested.

2. Ovarian stimulation (8 to 10 days)

  • What you do: inject yourself under the skin every day at a set time, and attend 2 to 4 monitoring visits for ultrasound and blood tests.
  • Why it's needed: in a normal cycle only one egg matures; stimulation brings a group of follicles along together.
  • How it feels: a full, bloated lower abdomen, tiredness, mood swings, breast tenderness and slight bruising at the injection sites.
  • Be careful: the ovaries are enlarged, so avoid vigorous exercise and sudden twisting movements to lower the risk of ovarian torsion.

3. Trigger injection (about 36 hours before egg retrieval)

  • What you do: inject on time, at exactly the time the clinic gives you.
  • Key point: this is the step where timing matters most — too early and the eggs aren't mature, too late and they may be released on their own. Set an alarm.

4. Egg retrieval (half a day)

  • Beforehand: fast as instructed (generally no food or drink in the 6 hours before the procedure), remove jewellery, contact lenses, nail polish and make-up, and arrange for someone to take you home. The male partner usually provides a sample on the same day.
  • The procedure: under anaesthesia or sedation, follicular fluid is collected through the vagina under ultrasound guidance, taking about 15 to 30 minutes.
  • How it feels: afterwards, lower abdominal bloating and pain, light bleeding and tiredness, which mostly ease within a few days.
  • Be prepared: the number of eggs collected is usually lower than the number of follicles seen on ultrasound. This is normal.

5. Fertilisation and embryo culture (3 to 5 days)

  • What you do: wait. The clinic will keep you updated.
  • What happens: egg and sperm are brought together (if sperm quality is poor, ICSI is used, where a single sperm is injected directly into the egg) → the embryos are cultured and graded → a decision is made on a day 3 or day 5 transfer → any remaining good-quality embryos are frozen.
  • Key point 1: grading reflects how an embryo looks; it doesn't mean its genes are normal.
  • Key point 2: the numbers shrink at every stage — not every egg collected is mature, not every mature egg fertilises, and not every fertilised egg becomes a usable embryo. This is the normal process and doesn't mean something has gone wrong.

6. Embryo transfer (about 15 minutes)

  • What you do: attend the clinic, have the transfer and go home the same day. Some clinics ask you to arrive with a moderately full bladder to help the ultrasound guidance.
  • The procedure: a fine catheter places the embryo into the uterine cavity through the cervix. It usually needs no anaesthesia and feels similar to a gynaecological examination.
  • Fresh versus frozen: if hormone levels are too high or there is a risk of ovarian hyperstimulation, the doctor will often recommend freezing all the embryos and arranging a frozen transfer at a later date.
  • Key point: you don't need ten days of bed rest. Normal routines are fine; just avoid vigorous exercise and heavy lifting. Staying in bed for long periods actually raises the risk of blood clots.

7. Luteal support (about 2 weeks)

  • What you do: use vaginal progesterone pessaries every day to support the uterine lining.
  • Key point: progesterone itself causes feelings similar to early pregnancy (breast tenderness, nausea, tiredness, discharge), so these feelings can't be used to tell whether it has worked. It's also best not to use a home pregnancy test early.
  • This is the hardest stretch: there's little for your body to do, but the psychological pressure is at its peak.

8. Pregnancy test

  • What you do: a blood test for hCG; if it's positive, you repeat it a few days later to see how the level is rising, and an ultrasound is then arranged to confirm.
  • If it doesn't work: go through this cycle's data with your doctor — response to medication, number of eggs retrieved, fertilisation rate, embryo quality — all of which is valuable for adjusting the plan next time. Your body and emotions both need time to recover, and rushing to start again isn't necessarily best.

About PGT: Do You Need Genetic Testing of Embryos?

PGT (preimplantation genetic testing) means taking a few cells from an embryo before transfer and analysing them genetically. It is the add-on most often recommended in recent years, and the one most worth getting clear on.

Three kinds of PGT, with completely different uses

TypeWhat it testsWhen it's used
PGT-MSingle-gene disordersCouples known to carry a gene for an inherited condition, such as thalassaemia or spinal muscular atrophy
PGT-SRStructural chromosome rearrangementsOne partner has a structural problem such as a translocation, often seen with recurrent miscarriage
PGT-AAbnormal chromosome numbers (aneuploidy)No known inherited condition; used to select embryos

The first two have clear medical indications. They are used to avoid passing a known genetic problem to the next generation, and who they are for is well defined.

The controversy centres on the third, PGT-A, because it is the one marketed widely to ordinary patients.

What the evidence says about PGT-A

The position of the major international professional bodies is fairly consistent: it is not recommended as routine testing for all IVF patients.

  • The American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) committee opinion of 2024 states that the value of PGT-A as routine screening for all IVF patients has not been proven; recent multicentre randomised controlled trials show similar overall pregnancy outcomes for frozen embryo transfer with or without PGT-A.
  • A large randomised trial published in the New England Journal of Medicine (Yan et al., 2021) compared 1,212 women aged 20 to 37 who had three or more good-quality blastocysts. The cumulative live birth rate within one year was 77.2% in the PGT-A group and 81.8% in the conventional IVF group — PGT-A brought no advantage.
  • The UK's HFEA rates PGT-A "red", meaning it may reduce the chance of a successful treatment.

The one fairly consistent positive signal is the miscarriage rate: in that trial the PGT-A group had a lower miscarriage rate (8.7% versus 12.6%). This doesn't contradict the live birth results — PGT-A screens out more embryos, so those left have a higher chance of implanting, but the total number available to transfer is smaller.

So when is it worth considering?

  • A clear genetic indication: PGT-M and PGT-SR have well-defined uses.
  • Recurrent miscarriage: if you want to reduce the chance of another miscarriage, it's worth discussing with your doctor.
  • Older age with a good ovarian response: ASRM considers this group may benefit more, but the evidence is still uncertain.

Where it's less suitable: people who have few embryos to begin with. If you only have one or two, the testing itself uses up embryos and adds a risk of damage, and an "abnormal" result may leave nothing to transfer — even though some embryos judged abnormal could in fact still lead to a pregnancy (mosaic embryos are a known problem).

Three practical questions to ask your doctor

  • Is there a clear medical indication in my case?
  • With the number of embryos I have now, could testing leave me with fewer embryos to transfer?
  • If the result comes back "mosaic", how does this clinic handle it?

In Hong Kong, assisted reproduction is regulated by the Human Reproductive Technology Ordinance. The scope of PGT and any limits that apply should be checked directly with a licensed centre.

Ovarian Hyperstimulation: Warning Signs You Need to Know

Ovarian hyperstimulation syndrome (OHSS) is a known risk of stimulation. Most cases are mild, but severe cases need immediate treatment.

If any of the following happens, contact your clinic straight away or go to A&E:

  • Severe abdominal pain and bloating, or your abdomen getting noticeably bigger in a short time
  • Rapid weight gain over a short period
  • Passing urine noticeably less often or in smaller amounts
  • Shortness of breath, or difficulty breathing when lying flat
  • Persistent vomiting, with no food or drink staying down
  • Pain and swelling in one calf (a blood clot needs to be ruled out)

These shouldn't wait until your next appointment, and shouldn't be handled with home remedies or over-the-counter medicine.

In the private sector, Hong Kong has a number of licensed assisted reproduction centres. Waiting times are shorter but costs are higher, and fees vary considerably, so it's best to ask each centre directly.

One practical consideration: the public-sector waits can add up to more than a year, and age itself is an important factor in outcomes. Whether to wait for public services has to be weighed against your own age and circumstances, and is worth discussing in detail with your doctor.

Where Can Chinese Medicine Fit In?

Many people see a Chinese medicine practitioner alongside IVF. There are a few practical considerations: preparation before a cycle, discomfort and emotions during treatment, and care with medication at each phase — for example, around egg retrieval there is a bleeding risk, so blood-invigorating herbs must be assessed by a practitioner.

The details are covered in a separate article (see How Does Chinese Medicine Work Alongside an IVF Cycle?). The most important principle is: make sure your fertility specialist and your Chinese medicine practitioner both know your full situation, and don't let preparation delay your treatment.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does an IVF cycle take?

About four to six weeks from the first injection to the pregnancy test. But once you add the initial tests and any waiting time, the whole journey is much longer. With a frozen embryo transfer cycle, the transfer is arranged in a later month.

Do the injections hurt? Can I give them myself?

The needles are very fine, and most people describe a slight sting. A nurse will show you how, and nearly everyone is confident within a few days. If you really can't inject yourself, a family member can help or you can go to the clinic.

Does egg retrieval hurt?

It is done under anaesthesia or sedation, so you generally don't feel pain during it. Afterwards, lower abdominal bloating and pain and some light bleeding are common, and these mostly ease within a few days.

How many eggs is a good number?

There's no "the more the better" answer. Too many actually raises the risk of ovarian hyperstimulation. Your doctor's aim is to collect a reasonable number of mature eggs, not to maximise the count.

Do I need to rest in bed after the transfer?

No, not for long periods. Normal routines are fine, just avoid vigorous exercise and heavy lifting. Staying in bed for a long time does no good and raises the risk of blood clots.

What does it feel like if the transfer has worked?

There's no reliable way to tell for yourself. Progesterone pessaries themselves cause feelings similar to early pregnancy, which makes it easy to misjudge. Go by the blood test result.

What's the difference between IUI and IVF?

The biggest difference is where fertilisation happens: with IUI, prepared sperm are placed directly into the uterine cavity and fertilisation still takes place inside the body, in the fallopian tube; with IVF, eggs are collected, fertilised in the lab, and the embryo is then placed back in the uterus. IUI needs no egg retrieval or anaesthesia, costs much less and is lighter on the body, but each cycle has a lower chance of success and needs open fallopian tubes.

How many IUI cycles should I do before moving to IVF?

For unexplained infertility, international guidelines generally suggest three to four cycles of ovulation-stimulating medication plus IUI before considering IVF, because most successes come in the first few cycles. But age is an important variable — if you're over 35, your doctor may suggest fewer cycles or going straight to IVF.

Will PGT-A definitely improve my chance of success?

Not necessarily. Recent large randomised controlled trials show that among patients with several good-quality blastocysts, the cumulative live birth rate is similar with or without PGT-A. The major international professional bodies do not recommend it as routine testing for all patients. There is a more consistent positive signal for miscarriage rates. Whether it suits you should be discussed in light of your specific medical indications and your number of embryos.

Does a high embryo grade mean the genes are normal?

No. The grade reflects how the embryo looks and how it is dividing, and doesn't necessarily say anything about whether its chromosomes are normal. An embryo that looks good can still have chromosome problems, and the reverse is also true.

If a cycle fails, how long do I wait before the next one?

That needs to be decided with your doctor based on how this cycle went. If you already have frozen embryos, a frozen transfer can usually be arranged fairly quickly; if you need to stimulate again, your body is generally given time to recover first. The timing should take into account both your physical condition and your age.

What does the male partner need to do?

Besides tests such as the semen analysis, lifestyle matters just as much — smoking, drinking, prolonged exposure to heat and lack of sleep can all affect sperm quality. The male partner usually needs to be there on the day of egg retrieval to give a sample, so it's best to confirm the arrangements beforehand.

Conclusion

The IVF process itself is quite regular: the busy part is concentrated in the 8 to 10 days of stimulation and the day of egg retrieval, and after that it is mostly waiting. What really wears people down is often not the body but the psychological pressure of the two weeks after transfer.

Knowing in advance what happens at each step, which sensations are normal and which are warning signs that need urgent care can remove a large share of unnecessary anxiety. When in doubt, ask your medical team directly — they have seen the same questions countless times.
