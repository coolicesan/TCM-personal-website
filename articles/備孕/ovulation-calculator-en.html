How Do You Calculate Your Ovulation Window?Which Days Are You Fertile?

Ovulation happens about 14 days before your next period, not 14 days after your last one. A Hong Kong registered Chinese medicine practitioner shows you how to work out your ovulation day and fertile window correctly, and explains why calculators are inaccurate if your cycle is irregular and what to do instead.

At a glanceOvulation generally happens about 14 days before your next period, not on day 14 after your last one. So the way to work it out is to take your cycle length and subtract 14. The fertile window is the roughly 5 days before ovulation plus the day of ovulation itself — about 6 days in all. If your cycle is irregular, this method isn't reliable, and you need to switch to ovulation test strips or observing cervical mucus.

Why Isn't It "Day 14"?

The idea of "ovulating on day 14" only holds for a 28-day cycle.

What actually happens is that the luteal phase — from ovulation to your next period — is relatively fixed in length, generally 12 to 16 days. Differences in cycle length come mainly from the follicular phase before ovulation.

So the right direction for working it out is to count back from your next period, not forward from this one.

How to Calculate It

The formula: cycle length − 14 = approximate ovulation day (counted from the first day of this period)

Your cycle lengthApproximate ovulation dayFertile window (approx.)
24 daysDay 10Days 5 to 10
26 daysDay 12Days 7 to 12
28 daysDay 14Days 9 to 14
30 daysDay 16Days 11 to 16
32 daysDay 18Days 13 to 18
35 daysDay 21Days 16 to 21

Where to start counting: the first day of bleeding of this period is day 1. Brown spotting before your period doesn't count.

Why Is the Fertile Window 6 Days?

Because sperm and egg survive for different lengths of time:

  • Sperm can survive for up to about 5 days in suitable cervical mucus
  • An egg survives for only about 12 to 24 hours after it is released

So the chance of conceiving is highest in the few days before ovulation plus the day of ovulation itself — by the time you have sex after ovulation, it is often already too late. This is one reason why many people "get the dates right but still get nothing".

Are Calculators and Apps Accurate?

What they do is really just the formula above, plus the average of your past cycles. So:

When they are more accurate

  • Your cycle is steady (varying by only a few days from month to month)
  • You've recorded the actual dates for at least three to six months

When they are unreliable

  • Your cycle is irregular, or varies by more than a week from month to month
  • You've just come off the contraceptive pill, are postpartum, are breastfeeding, or are around the menopause
  • You've recently been under a lot of stress, sleeping badly, had a marked change in weight or taken a long-haul trip
  • Conditions with unstable ovulation such as PMOS (see PMOS (Formerly PCOS))

The key limitation: an app's "ovulation day" is a calculated value, not a measured one. It simply guesses from your earlier cycles and has no idea what is actually happening this month. The more irregular your cycle, the worse the guess.

What to Do If Your Cycle Is Irregular

Drop pure calculation and use methods that reflect what is happening now:

  • Observing cervical mucus: near ovulation, discharge becomes clear, thin and stretchy, like raw egg white. It's free, and shows changes in real time.
  • Ovulation test strips: they detect the LH surge, and ovulation generally follows about 24 to 36 hours after the peak.
  • Basal body temperature: it can't predict, but it can confirm afterwards whether ovulation really happened, and how long your luteal phase is.

For how to use all three properly and where they trip people up, see How Do You Know If You're Ovulating?

Can the "Safe Period" Be Used for Contraception?

Not recommended. Contraception by calculation has a fairly high failure rate, for exactly the reason given above — the timing of ovulation moves with stress, illness and changes of routine, and sperm can survive in the body for several days. There is no such thing as an absolutely "safe period". If you need contraception, discuss a reliable method with your doctor.

When to See a Doctor

  • Cycles persistently shorter than 24 days or longer than 38 days (see How Long Should a Normal Menstrual Cycle Be?)
  • Fewer than 8 periods a year, or no period for more than three months
  • No signs of ovulation for several cycles in a row
  • Not pregnant after a year of trying (six months if you're 35 or over) (see Why Can't I Get Pregnant?)

Frequently Asked Questions

My cycle isn't fixed — which number should I use?

If you must calculate, take the shortest cycle length from the last three to six months minus 14 as the start of your fertile window, and the longest cycle length minus 14 as the end, which gives a wider range. A more practical approach, though, is to switch to ovulation test strips or observing cervical mucus.

How often should we have sex during the fertile window?

Generally once every one to two days during the fertile window is enough. More often isn't necessarily better, while too much restraint may mean missing the timing. Rather than working out the dates exactly, keep up a regular pattern throughout the fertile window.

Does ovulation pain mean I'm ovulating?

Some women feel a mild distension or twinge on one side of the lower abdomen around ovulation, which can be a guide, but not everyone has it and it isn't precise enough to rely on alone.

Is a little bleeding around ovulation normal?

Some women get very light spotting around ovulation, usually linked to a brief drop in oestrogen at that time. But if it keeps happening, is heavier, or comes with abdominal pain, it should be checked.

Can Chinese medicine help with ovulation problems?

If irregular ovulation comes from constitution, mood or sleep factors, Chinese medicine can work on it by regulating the menstrual cycle. But if PMOS, a thyroid problem or the like is involved, get tested and diagnosed first, then decide the direction of care — find out the cause first, then work on your health, and the order shouldn't be reversed.

Conclusion

The most common mistake in working out ovulation is counting forward 14 days from this period. The right way is to subtract 14 from your cycle length, and to understand the fertile window as the roughly 5 days before ovulation plus the day itself.

But calculation has its limits: it assumes your cycle will repeat past patterns. If your cycle isn't stable, no calculator can help — switching to ovulation test strips or observing cervical mucus will be far more practical than carrying on calculating.
